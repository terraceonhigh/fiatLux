# The National Loaf — the actual specification

*Pulled 2026-08-12 for Nona's recipe chapter. **Primary source in hand**: "Specifications for National Flour", *Nature* **147**, 665–666, No. 3735, 31 May 1941 — filed at [`sources/nature-1941-specifications-for-national-flour.pdf`](../sources/nature-1941-specifications-for-national-flour.pdf), retrieved through UBC access. Figures below are data from that paper; the prose is paraphrase, with short quotes marked.*

*Supplementary: Hansard answers 1943–1953, and D. W. Kent-Jones, "The case for fortified flour", *Proc. Nutr. Soc.* 1958 — for what happened after 1941. Sources listed at the end.*

---

## Who wrote it

**The Accessory Food Factors Committee of the Lister Institute and the Medical Research Council.** A memorandum in the summer of 1940 recommended 80–85 per cent extraction; the Committee met again on **23 January 1941** and settled on 85. The Ministry of Food then adopted it.

**So the specification originated with a scientific committee and was made compulsory by a ministry.** Those are two separate acts by two separate bodies, and the book already runs that shape — the council sets priorities competently and the register does the harm.

## The specification

**Extraction: 85 per cent.** The white-flour baseline in this paper is **73 per cent**, not 70 or 72.

**Three nutritional aims, in the Committee's order:**

1. As much of the B vitamins as possible — riboflavin, nicotinic acid, **especially B₁**.
2. As much protein as possible, **including the protein in the outer layers**, which is of higher nutritive value than that in white flour.
3. **As little bran as possible.** The stated reason is that wartime diets already contained much vegetable food with a lot of roughage, and increasing it further was undesirable.

**That third aim is the one worth having.** The specification is not maximising wholesomeness — it is **limiting bran on account of what else is already on the table.** A document that accounts for the rest of the diet.

**Hard numeric requirements:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Vitamin B₁ | **not less than 1 I.U. per gram** (thiochrome method) |
| Fibre | **less than 0.9 per cent**, assuming 15 per cent water content |

## Two production routes, and a transition plan

Flour of 85 per cent extraction could be had two ways:

- **Flour (1) — "straight-run".** Re-arrange the milling machinery to yield 85 per cent of the wheat directly. Defined as 85 per cent by weight of the clean wheat, containing the **maximum** of germ and aleurone layer and the **minimum** of pericarp.
- **Flour (2) — reconstituted.** Take ordinary white flour at 73 per cent and add back **"all the reduction roll tails, mill finish and purifier tails and sufficient fine bran"** to reach 85 per cent, excluding the bran and **pollards**.

**Both were judged of equal nutritive value.** Flour (1) won on three grounds: highest vitamin B content of any flour at that extraction from the same grist, lowest fibre, and — the deciding one — **the loaf that most closely resembled white bread in flavour and texture.** The paper records that its colour was pale brown and *"was not unattractive,"* and that on technical and aesthetic grounds the loaf was considered excellent.

**Two things fall out of that.**

**At equal nutrition, the tiebreak was how much the bread looked and tasted like white bread.** Stated openly, as a design criterion, by a nutrition committee.

**And the recommendation shipped with an interim.** Because re-arranging mill machinery takes time, the Committee recommended adopting **flour (2) immediately** — producible with existing plant — and moving to flour (1) when the mills could be converted. **A specification with a "do this now, do the right thing when the machinery allows" clause**, which is exactly the shape of a post-collapse document.

## Comparative analysis, all four from the same grist, 15% water

| Flour | Fibre % | Ash % | Protein % | Vitamin B₁ I.U./g | Calcium mg% | Phosphorus, total mg% | Phosphorus as phytic acid mg% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 (85%)** straight-run | 0.60 | 0.90 | 11.4 | 1.2 | 27 | 203 | 123 |
| **2 (85%)** reconstituted | 0.85 | 0.94 | 11.5 | 1.15 | 27 | 211 | 128 |
| **White (73%)** | 0 to 0.20 | 0.46 | 10.6 | 0.35 | 15 | 101 | 35 |
| **Wholemeal (100%)** | 1.8 | 1.51 | 11.9 | 1.4 | 36 | 343 | 246 |

**Note the phytic acid column.** It nearly quadruples from white to 85 per cent, and doubles again to wholemeal. That column is why the calcium exists.

## Calcium — and a correction

**I had this wrong yesterday**, working from search summaries: I recorded 7 oz per sack rising to 14 oz over the post-war years. **That is not what happened.** They are two different rates for two different flours, set at the same time:

| Flour | Calcium needed | Creta praeparata B.P. per 280 lb sack |
|---|---|---|
| **White** | 65 mg Ca / 100 g | **7 oz** |
| **85 per cent extraction** | 130 mg Ca / 100 g | **14 oz** |

**The extra 7 oz exists solely to neutralise the extra phytic acid the higher-extraction flour brings with it.**

**That is the whole thing, and it is now precise rather than gestural.** The healthier bread carries an antinutrient that binds calcium — from the bread *and from the rest of the diet* — so **the better loaf requires exactly twice the chalk, and half of that chalk does nothing except cancel out a harm the improvement introduced.**

### Extrapolating the dose to wholemeal — for *Fortification*

**The chapter's formula specifies wholemeal, not 85% extraction**, so the spec's 14 oz does not apply. Derived from the paper's own logic and its own phytate figures:

| | Phytic-acid P, mg% | Increment over white | Corrective creta |
|---|---|---|---|
| White (73%) | 35 | — | base **7 oz** |
| 85% extraction | 123 | +88 | +7 oz → **14 oz** |
| **Wholemeal (100%)** | **246** | **+211** | **+16.8 oz → ~24 oz** |

**~24 oz creta praeparata per 280 lb sack = 0.53% of flour weight**, rounded to **0.5%** in the formula. Cross-checks via the calcium route at 221 mg Ca per 100 g against white's 65.

**Sanity check it passes:** the paper notes the trade was already adding acid calcium phosphate at 12 oz per sack, and up to 4½ lb per sack in some self-raising flours — so 24 oz is well inside what bakers were already putting in flour.

*Two knock-ons of choosing wholemeal that the formula does not yet address: it drinks more water than 85% flour, so 66% hydration is on the low side; and it carries more germ, so it keeps worse than the spec's already-unmeasured 4–6 weeks.*

The dosage was calculated from MRC tests on human subjects **who took about half their total calories as bread, with only small amounts of milk and very little cheese.** So the figure is calibrated to a diet where bread is half of everything — which is the diet this book's campus is heading toward.

**And the trade objection was already dead.** Bakers had long added comparable amounts of 'improvers' — acid calcium phosphate at 12 oz per sack, and up to 4½ lb per sack in some self-raising flours then on the market. **Adding chalk to bread was not a novelty; it was a smaller intervention than the trade was already making.**

## Iron was considered and rejected

Anaemia from dietary iron shortage was common and would have been cheap to prevent with simple ferrous salts. **The Committee declined**, on the ground that added iron salts might destroy other nutrients present, and recommended no addition until more was known.

**So the 1941 specification deliberately omits iron.** It went in later — the **Conference on the Post-War Loaf (1945)** set the "token" levels at, per 100 g flour: **thiamine 0.24 mg, nicotinic acid 1.60 mg, iron 1.65 mg.** A reversal on better evidence, four years on.

## Keeping properties — an estimate, not a measurement

The paper is candid: **no exact evidence was available.** From experience with other brown and wholemeal flours, the Committee judged that 85 per cent flour could keep in good condition **4–6 weeks or longer** under reasonable storage.

**A specification with a load-bearing number nobody had measured.** The germ's oil is what limits it, and a flour that keeps six weeks instead of months is a *distribution* constraint, not a nutrition one.

## How it ended

The **Cohen Committee** reported in 1956 that it was not persuaded of any danger from fortified white flour as against longer-extraction flour. The National Loaf was abolished the same year. **Mandated by a document; killed by a document.**

Kent-Jones's aside is the sentence that matters for this book: *"In pre-war days also, longer extraction flour was made compulsory and improvement in health soon took place."* **Compulsion is the active ingredient.** Everything else on the specification is chemistry.

## What Nona can and cannot write

**She can write all of it.** Extraction rate, fibre ceiling, B₁ floor, the chalk dosage, the mill fractions, the interim clause, the keeping estimate. None of it needs equipment she lacks — the *knowledge* is the artifact.

**Three things she cannot write:**

1. **"In the proportions authorised."** The phrase is in the 1943 Hansard answer describing the permitted ingredients. **A specification that defers to an authorisation held elsewhere** — and there is no elsewhere.
2. **The compulsion clause.** White bread was *banned*. Nona has no power to ban anything; she is the woman who could not get into a clinic without Collie spending standing on her.
3. **The chalk.** Creta praeparata at 14 oz per 280 lb sack is a *procurement* line. Ground limestone or crushed shell would substitute in principle — and would want the degraded-version treatment, because purity and dose are the whole point of a fortificant.

**And the structural consequence for the sequence:** she drafts the correct answer in ch3, and the deficiency ward fills anyway in *Boil Water*. Not because she was wrong. **Because a draft is not a policy**, and *Druzhina* — where the institution learns it can compel things — is one chapter too late for the boys who dug.
