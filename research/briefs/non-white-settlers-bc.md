# Non-White Settlers in British Columbia

*Research memo. Real-world facts with sources, followed by hooks into the
manuscript. The facts are evidence, not canon. Every hook is `MODEL` until
Terrace decides otherwise.*

Scope: South Asian, Chinese, Japanese, Black, and Hawaiian settlement in BC,
roughly 1858–1949, with Washington parallels left for later. Indigenous
nations are not settlers. They appear here only where their history is
entangled with these groups.

---

## I. South Asians and Sikhs

- About 5,000 South Asians, about 90% of them Sikh, had settled in BC by 1908.
  They worked mainly in lumber mills, logging, and construction. By 1924, 609
  of 680 South Asians in BC worked in the mills. [SACH]
- The Khalsa Diwan Society formed in 1906. Its gurdwara at 1866 West 2nd
  Avenue, Kitsilano, opened in 1908 and served the millworkers near the False
  Creek lumber mills. It was the centre of spiritual, political, social, and
  economic life for Indians of all faiths. Its kitchen served langar daily to
  anyone, whatever their faith, and it worked as a job exchange and message
  point. It was sold in 1970 to fund the Ross Street Gurdwara. [VHF]
- South Asians lost the Vancouver municipal vote in 1907 and regained the
  franchise in 1947. [SACH]
- The 1908 continuous-journey regulation required arrival by direct passage
  from India, which no route offered. [CSH; Canada.ca]
- *Komagata Maru*, 1914: 376 passengers (340 Sikh, 24 Muslim, 12 Hindu)
  arrived May 23 and were held in the harbour until July 23. Only about 20
  returning residents were admitted. A Shore Committee of 15 local Indians
  raised $5,000 at once, pledged $66,000 more, and sent several tonnes of food
  to the ship. At Budge Budge, near Calcutta, 20 passengers were killed and
  202 were jailed. Canada apologized in 2016. [CSH; Canada.ca]
- Paldi, on Vancouver Island, was founded in 1917 by Mayo Singh as a sawmill
  town. Its workforce was Sikh, Japanese, Chinese, and white. The gurdwara
  dates from 1919 and is still active. [CBC Paldi]
- In 1942, the IWA won equal pay for Asian millworkers. [SACH]

## II. Chinese

- CPR construction, 1880–85: about 7,000 Chinese workers arrived for it, and
  they made up about three-quarters of the railway workforce. Estimates of
  deaths range from 600 to 2,200. [Canada.ca]
- Head tax: $50 in 1885, $100 in 1900–01, $500 in 1903, about two years'
  wages. Over 97,000 people paid about $23 million, and BC received roughly
  40% of it. [BC Gov]
- The 1923 Chinese Immigration Act largely ended Chinese immigration. The
  community called its Dominion Day passage "Humiliation Day." Canada
  apologized for the head tax in 2006. [Canada.ca]
- Chinese farmers could not buy Crown land. They leased land on Musqueam
  Reserve 2 for market gardens, with 18 leases by 1917. The gardens fed the
  Lower Mainland. Musqueam–Chinese marriages followed. Agnes Grant lost her
  Indian Act status when she married Hong Tim Hing. [Spheres; HistoricPlaces]
- In 1903, Edmund A. Smith built a fish-butchering machine that displaced
  Chinese cannery butchers, and he named it with a slur for them. See
  `world/geography.md`. [HistoryLink]

## III. Japanese

- The 1907 anti-Asian riot in Vancouver damaged Chinatown and Japantown.
  [JCH]
- 1900–1930: salmon "conservation" policy was used to cut Japanese Canadian
  fishing licences. A 1928 Supreme Court ruling held that the province could
  not favour some British subjects over others. [UBC Migration]
- Beginning December 8, 1941, the navy impounded about 1,200 Japanese
  Canadian boats at Annieville Dyke on the Fraser and sold them. Accounts vary
  from about 1,000 to 1,200. [JCH; VIA]
- February 24, 1942: Order in Council PC 1486 authorized removal. More than
  21,000 people were uprooted from the coast, and Hastings Park was used as a
  holding site. After 1945, some were exiled to Japan. The Redress Agreement
  was signed on September 22, 1988. [JCH; Canada.ca]

## IV. Black settlers

- In 1858, Governor James Douglas, himself of mixed ancestry, invited San
  Francisco's Black community to Vancouver Island. Several hundred came and
  settled in Victoria, Saanich, and Salt Spring Island. Refused entry to the
  volunteer fire department, they formed the Pioneer Rifle Corps in 1861, and
  many returned to the US after 1865. [BC Leg]
- Hogan's Alley was the centre of Strathcona's Black neighbourhood. Council
  approved redevelopment in 1958, and the viaduct project destroyed the
  neighbourhood along with parts of Chinatown. [BCBHAS]

## V. Hawaiians (Kānaka)

- Hawaiians arrived from 1787 and gave the Hudson's Bay Company more than
  3,000 man-years of labour. Their settlements include Kanaka Ranch (now Coal
  Harbour), Kanaka Creek (Maple Ridge), and Salt Spring Island. They married
  into Indigenous communities. [Can Geo]

## VI. Recurring mechanisms

`MODEL` These recur across the groups above:

- Exclusion by licence rather than by ban: fishing licences, land purchase,
  water rights, and the franchise.
- Labour hired through co-ethnic contractors, which kept the employer at one
  remove.
- A named job becomes the group's name, as with the butchering machine.
- Mutual aid that survives because it never depended on the state: langar,
  the Shore Committee, the Musqueam–Chinese leases.
- Settler-on-settler relief resting on Indigenous land: the Musqueam leases.
