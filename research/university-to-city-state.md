# The Reactivation

### How a Cascadian university's ordinary functions generalize into state capacity during a long decay

*Research brief. Confidence notes at the end.*

---

## Thesis: nothing is invented, everything is reactivated

The instinct is to ask how a university *acquires* state functions. That's backwards, and getting it backwards will cost you the best material in the setting.

The medieval university was a self-governing corporation that already held sovereign powers, and the modern university is the demilitarized residue of one. What it held:

- **Its own courts and its own jurisdiction.** *Privilegium fori* — masters and scholars were tried by the university, not by the town or the ordinary courts. Campus Security becoming a gendarmerie is not a new function. It is a restored one.
- **Price and rent control over its host city.** The *taxatio* — the university set the rents that townspeople could charge students, and enforced it.
- **The power to secede, and to take the economy with it.** The Paris dispersion of 1229–31; the scholars who walked out of Oxford in 1209 and founded Cambridge. A university on strike was an economic weapon.
- **Armed conflict with its host, and expanded jurisdiction as the settlement.** Oxford's St Scholastica Day riot of 1355 ran for days and killed scores. The Crown's settlement *expanded the university's authority over the town* and left the mayor annually penitent for centuries. Your premise is attested, six hundred years early.

So the frame for the whole setting: **the state decays, and the functions the state most recently absorbed from the university are the first to come back.** Policing, courts, price-setting, licensing, weights and measures, the census, the school system, the granary. Nobody in your story declares a republic. They restore a privilege, and the restoration is legal, gradual, and mostly conducted in committee.

This also tells you the *order of events*. Functions revert roughly in reverse chronology of when the state took them.

---

## The mechanism: five ways a function generalizes

A mapping of departments to ministries is a glossary. What you want is the mechanism, because then you can generalize any function you haven't thought of yet.

| Mechanism | Shape | Example | Feels like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scope creep** | Same service, wider population, no decision ever made | Health Services starts treating non-students → becomes the public health authority | Nothing. This is the invisible one. |
| **Vertical integration** | The supplier fails, so buy it, or seize it | Food Services → its regional contracts → the farm itself | Prudent management |
| **Absorption** | Hire the collapsed agency's people, inherit its function with them | The municipal water engineer joins Facilities because his utility can't make payroll | Charity, then indispensability |
| **Stretched authority** | An existing bylaw read far past its intent, because the alternative is nothing | Student conduct code → criminal law. No new law written. | Lawyering |
| **Monopoly by survival** | Last working one in the region | The press, the machine shop, the assay lab, the autoclave | Reluctant obligation |

**"Stretched authority" is the engine of the good version of this story.** State-building here looks like a legal opinion, not a revolution. Someone reads §14 of the University Act in a way it was never meant to be read; General Counsel writes a memo saying it's defensible; the committee votes yes because the alternative is to do nothing while people die. Ten years later that memo is constitutional law and nobody can find the original file.

The five mechanisms also have different *reversibility*, which matters for the frictions section. Scope creep can be quietly walked back. Absorption cannot — you now employ the person and owe them a pension.

# Part I — The named functions, in depth

## Campus Security → the gendarmerie, and the two forces inside it

**Starting position matters and is unglamorous.** Campus security at a Canadian university is typically *not* police: unarmed, non-sworn, powers of a private citizen plus trespass authority under a property act, radio-dispatched, and mostly doing lockups, parking, first aid, escorts and lost keys. At UBC there is an actual RCMP detachment on campus; at UVic there is not. So the starting capability is low, which is good — the arc is longer.

The path:

1. **Mutual aid, then dependence.** Response times from the municipal force lengthen. Campus Security starts holding scenes for hours instead of minutes, then starts resolving them.
2. **Deputization or its equivalent.** Special-constable status, or a provincial designation, granted in an emergency and never withdrawn because the granting authority stops existing. This is the legal hinge and it should be a boring document.
3. **Absorption.** Municipal officers arrive as individuals when their pension fund evaporates. They bring training, equipment, and a *different culture* — sworn officers who have used force, joining a service whose senior people have not. That culture clash is a whole subplot.
4. **The first killing.** The institutional crisis is not the death. It's that there is no external body to investigate it. The university has to invent its own inquest, and whatever it invents becomes the constitution's criminal procedure.

**Then it splits into two forces, and you should keep them distinct:**

- **The Proctors** — internal, low-violence, continuous with student conduct enforcement. They handle citizens. Their authority is disciplinary and their punishments are academic and social: suspension, exclusion, loss of standing. Enormously powerful, because in this polity exclusion means no ration card.
- **The Field Force** — perimeter, checkpoints, convoy escort, revenue collection in the hinterland. They handle everyone else. Different uniform, different recruitment, worse reputation.

Two details worth stealing:

- **The night-escort service becomes the intelligence service.** Walk-safe programs exist to accompany people across campus at night. Doing that at scale means knowing who is where, with whom, at what hour, and writing it down. Nobody designs this as surveillance; it is surveillance within five years.
- **Keys.** Campus Security shares custody of the master key system with Facilities. In a polity whose crown jewels are a culture collection, a steam plant and an archive, physical key control *is* the security apparatus, and the locksmith is a senior official.

## Senate and the Board → the legislature, and the quorum crisis

Universities already have something close to a bicameral constitution, and it is a genuine separation of powers:

- **Board of Governors** — property, finance, fiduciary duty. Crucially, its seats are substantially **filled by government appointment.**
- **Senate** — academic policy, curriculum, admission standards, degrees, libraries. Elected faculty, elected students, deans ex officio.

**The quorum crisis is the founding event, and it is a legal problem rather than a dramatic one — which is why it's good.** A university is a corporation chartered by a legislature. Government-appointed Board seats simply stop being filled; nobody rescinds anything. Quorum fails. The corporation is legally adrift while remaining physically intact, fully staffed, and operating normally. There is no moment of rupture. There is a meeting that cannot legally be held.

What happens next is the constitutional question of your setting, and there are three clean answers:

- **Senate absorbs the Board's powers** by resolution, on the argument that a body cannot be left without a governor. Legitimate, slow, deliberative, and structurally incapable of fighting a war.
- **A President's Emergency Committee** takes executive power and keeps Senate alive as the thing that ratifies it. This is a junta with a legitimating assembly, which is what most of them are.
- **The legal opinion.** General Counsel writes that the corporation's powers survive the chartering authority's lapse and vest in the remaining seated members. **The founding document of the university-state is a memo.** Cite the Hudson's Bay Company for precedent if you want a real one — a chartered corporation that governed Rupert's Land because the chartering power could not reach it, and whose Fort Victoria post is, inconveniently for your sense of subtlety, exactly where this story is set.

**The committee structure is the machinery of state, already built:**

| University body | Becomes |
|---|---|
| Admissions | Citizenship and immigration |
| Curriculum Committee | The licensing state — who may practice what |
| Awards and Financial Aid | Welfare and pensions |
| Appeals / Senate Appeals | Supreme court |
| Tenure and Promotion | The mechanism of aristocracy |
| Research Ethics Board | The thing that says no, and is hated for it |
| Space Allocation | Land law |

And **Robert's Rules of Order survives as constitutional practice** — points of order, motions to table, the tyranny of the person who knows procedure best. A polity governed by parliamentary procedure written for faculty meetings is both funny and plausible, and it produces a specific kind of villain.

**The Registrar becomes the most powerful office in the state, and almost nobody in your story will notice until it's too late.** The Registrar holds the rolls: who exists, what they're qualified to do, who is in good standing. Student number becomes citizen number. Transcript becomes the license to practice. Convocation becomes enfranchisement. Expulsion becomes exile. Records officers are the interior ministry.

## The Library → the Chancery

The Library's generalization is the least obvious and the most important, because it is the institution that decides what the next generation is *able* to know.

**What it already does that is a state function:** provenance and chain of custody, restricted-access reading rooms, climate-controlled storage, classification schemes, interlibrary loan, and — in Archives and Special Collections — the legal custody of records with retention schedules and access restrictions. That is a chancery. It only lacks a seal.

The functions it generalizes into:

- **The technical reference service.** The single highest-value output. Not books — *tables*. Solubilities, dosages, pharmacopoeial monographs, structural spans, alloy compositions, sensitivity charts. Re-deriving a table empirically costs years and patients. Interlibrary loan becomes **circuit riders**: copyists who ride out to satellite towns with the sections those towns need, and ride back with what those towns learned.
- **The law.** Statutes, case reports, precedent, and the memos. The Library is where the memo from the quorum crisis is kept, which means the Library can produce or fail to produce the state's founding document.
- **The mint's reference shelf.** Assay tables, standards, weights and measures.
- **Classification, therefore censorship.** Restricted access already exists as a preservation practice. The distance between "this item is fragile and requires supervision" and "this item is restricted" is one meeting.

**Preservation triage is the Library's tragedy, and it should be dramatized:**

- **Digital dies first and dies completely.** Licensed databases go dark the moment a subscription lapses or a server fails — and much of a modern research library's content was never *owned*, only licensed. The serials cancellation is a literal historical event that becomes an amnesia. Formats rot, drives fail, nobody can fab a replacement controller. **The first great forgetting is silent, and it happens while the buildings are still fully functional and the lights are still on.**
- **Paper is acid.** Twentieth-century wood-pulp stock self-destructs. Deacidification is chemistry the Chemistry department can do — at a cost, in a queue, with a triage list. Somebody writes that list. Somebody is on the committee that decides the Slavic collection is not on it.
- **Microfilm is the winner, and this is genuinely counterintuitive.** Silver-halide film on a polyester base, properly processed and stored, is readable by eye and light for centuries and needs no machine to decode. It's makeable — silver, gelatin, a darkroom. A city-state that deliberately films its core collection has performed the single highest-leverage act of long-term statecraft available to it, and it will be argued about bitterly because filming costs silver that the hospital wants.
- **Real case:** Sarajevo's National and University Library burned in August 1992 while the university continued to teach through the siege. Both halves of that are your setting.

## Dining Hall / Food Services → the commissariat, and the real seat of legitimacy

Deeply unglamorous and possibly the most powerful department in the university-state.

**What it already has:** bulk procurement contracts with regional producers, industrial kitchens capable of tens of thousands of covers a day, walk-in refrigeration, dish sanitation at scale, allergen and dietary tracking, and — the crucial one — **meal plans, which are ration cards with a swipe.**

The generalizations:

- **The meal plan becomes the ration card, and therefore Food Services holds the second census.** Cross-referenced against the Registrar's roll, it is the state's actual population data — not who is enrolled but who *ate yesterday*. Discrepancies between the two rolls are how the state discovers ghost citizens, hoarders, refugees and the dead. Two departments holding two lists that don't match is a permanent institutional feud and an excellent engine of plot.
- **In-housing, then vertical integration.** The contract caterer's parent company stops existing. The university takes the operation in-house, and its procurement office — which already knows every farmer in the valley by name and volume — becomes the agriculture ministry's purchasing arm, then its planning arm, then its landlord.
- **The central kitchen is the most important building on campus.** Not the library, not the hospital. Bread is made in one place, at scale, by a small number of people who cannot be replaced quickly, using fuel that must arrive daily.
- **Legitimacy is manufactured at the servery.** Nobody revolts while fed. The queue is where the polity is visible to itself every single day, and any change to the ration is a political act that 20,000 people experience simultaneously within one hour. The dining hall is where your riot starts.

Also: the walk-in freezers and the sanitation chemistry belong to the same cold-and-chlorine supply chain as the pharmacy. Food Services and the hospital are competing for the same ammonia plant.

## Botany, the Herbarium and the Garden → the agriculture ministry and the pharmacy

The template here is real and should be the spine: **agricultural extension.** The land-grant tradition and the Wisconsin Idea produced universities that already believed teaching and governing the countryside was their institutional purpose — county agents, demonstration plots, soil testing, published bulletins for farmers. A university with an extension tradition doesn't have to invent a relationship with its hinterland. It has one, with an office and a budget line.

Worth knowing for campus choice: in Washington, **WSU is the land-grant and UW is not.** UBC has a Faculty of Land and Food Systems and a working farm of some tens of hectares. That asymmetry is a real setting constraint.

Functions:

- **The seed bank and the seed distribution.** Selection, cleaning, storage, and the annual allocation to villages. Whoever controls seed controls the next harvest, which is a harder power than controlling this one.
- **Plant pathology = famine prevention.** In a wet maritime climate, crop disease is fungal and constant. A department that can identify a blight and recommend a rotation is worth more to the hinterland than the militia is.
- **The herbarium** — the reference collection that lets you know what a plant *is*, which is the difference between digitalis and a poisoning.
- **The medicinal garden**, feeding directly into the pharmacopeia: poppy, foxglove, belladonna, willow. Standardization is the hard part, and it ties Botany to the assay lab. See the penicillin brief.
- **Soil testing, and the survey.** Which becomes cadastre, which becomes land tax.
- **The extension service becomes the state's presence in the countryside** — often the only representative of the capital a village ever sees, and therefore the face of the regime. Agronomists are ambassadors and, inevitably, informants.

**The greenhouses are the fragile crown jewel:** glass, heat, and continuous attention. They are how you keep a strain alive over a bad winter and how you propagate anything at scale. They break in windstorms. Glaziers are a strategic profession.

## Facilities / Physical Plant → the actual government

Not on your list, and it should be at the top of it.

Universities own a piece of civic infrastructure that is genuinely rare: **a district energy system.** A central plant — historically steam, increasingly hot water — distributing heat through miles of buried mains to every building, often with its own substation, its own water and sewer trunks, its own fleet, its own machine shop, and its own bore holes. UBC runs hot-water district energy with a wood-waste bioenergy facility as part of the supply, which in a forested region is a plausible power core that does not depend on a distant dam.

So Facilities holds: heat, water, sewage, electrical distribution, the vehicle fleet, the fuel yard, the machine shop, the grounds, and the keys. Every single item on that list is a sovereign function.

**The prestige inversion is the most useful social fact in this brief.** In the old university, the Chief Facilities Officer reported to a Vice-President and was invisible. In the university-state, **the Chief Engineer outranks any Dean**, because the Dean's output is optional and the Chief Engineer's is not. The machinist outranks the postdoc. The steamfitter's apprenticeship is more competitive than the doctoral program. The people who spent careers being condescended to are now the people who allocate heat, and some of them are gracious about it and some are absolutely not. That resentment, arriving with power, is the best character material in the setting.

Corollary: deferred maintenance is the state's true balance sheet. Every year of austerity in phase one is a debt that comes due in phase four, and the roof that fails is the roof over the herbarium.

# Part II — The second tier, compressed

- **Health Services and the Faculty of Medicine → hospital plus public health authority.** Quarantine, vaccination, water and food inspection, death registration. Public health is where state coercion is most legitimate and most expandable — a quarantine power is a general detention power with a doctor's signature. A teaching hospital with a trauma centre is a regional monopoly worth going to war over.
- **Nursing → the largest immediately-useful professional cohort in the institution**, and therefore the profession that gets organized, licensed, and politically weighty first.
- **Engineering → the works ministry.** Machine shop, foundry, materials testing, surveying, water works. Also: undergraduate engineering culture already runs on guild logic — initiation, insignia, stunts, inter-faculty rivalry, a jealously defended trade identity. It becomes a guild without changing much.
- **Chemistry → the pharmacopeia, the assay office, and the explosives.** All three, and the same people, which is a governance problem.
- **Law → the courts.** Legal clinics already provide the service; moot court becomes court; the tension between the professors who want a coherent legal system and the executive that wants outcomes is a permanent front.
- **Athletics and Recreation → the militia, the reservoir, and the assembly ground.** Gyms as barracks and hospital overflow; pools as stored water (and a chlorine dependency); playing fields as farmland; the stadium as the place a population physically assembles, which makes it the site of the popular assembly whether the charter recognizes one or not. Varsity squads convert to units with existing cohesion and existing rivalries.
- **The University Press and the Bookstore → the printing office.** Bureaucracy runs on forms; forms need a press. Whoever holds the press holds the ration cards, the currency, the statutes and the news. Typesetting and binding become state trades.
- **Development and Alumni Relations → the foreign ministry.** Genuinely non-obvious and genuinely good: the alumni network is a pre-existing diaspora with a sentimental obligation to the institution, distributed across every surviving settlement in the region, already catalogued by name, location and capacity to give. The office whose entire professional skill is extracting resources from distant sympathizers becomes the office that negotiates tribute, safe passage and treaties. Fundraisers make excellent diplomats and the transition requires no retraining at all.
- **Institutional Research and Planning → the statistical office.** Enrollment projection becomes population projection becomes food planning.
- **Student Housing and Residence Life → the municipality.** Wardens, ward bosses, and Residence Advisors as the lowest and most intimate tier of government. This is, in fact, how it already works.
- **Continuing Studies and Extension → the school system for the entire region**, and the ministry of information.
- **Computer Science → collapses hardest, then reinvents itself as the numerical bureau.** No fabs, no replacement parts, a decade or two of hoarded machines and then nothing. What survives is the *discipline*: people trained to think in algorithms, doing ration optimization, ballistics, actuarial tables and census arithmetic by hand, slide rule and mechanical calculator. The department that was the most modern becomes the most archaic, and its members know it.
- **The Museum of Anthropology and the archaeology collections → the legitimating past, and a live sovereignty dispute.** Holding other peoples' ancestors and belongings was already contested. With no federal repatriation framework and no Crown to appeal to, the collection becomes a bargaining chip in negotiations with the actual co-sovereigns next door — see below. Handle this as politics, not as set dressing.
- **Classics, Philosophy, the Fine Arts → the hardest question the setting asks.** They have tenure, budget lines, and no survival function. Cutting them is the moment the university admits it is no longer a university. Not cutting them is the argument that it must remain one to be worth defending. Don't resolve it.

# Part III — The Cascadian specifics

## Which campus, and why it decides what kind of story this is

Food arithmetic picks your setting, so state it early and let it drive everything.

| | Population problem | Geography | Distinctive assets | Story it produces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **UVic** (recommended) | ~22k campus, metro on the order of 400k | Island. Water boundary. Saanich Peninsula and Cowichan Valley farmland immediately adjacent | Victoria is **already a capital** — legislature, archives, courts, bureaucrats. Naval base at Esquimalt. Ferry terminals | **The clean city-state.** Defensible, feedable, and it inherits a real capital's furniture |
| **UBC** | ~2.6M metro on the far side of the chokepoint | Point Grey peninsula, essentially one land approach | Working farm, botanical garden, district energy with biomass, teaching hospital, a cyclotron nobody can power | **The wealthy enclave with a hostile hinterland.** The wall is the whole plot |
| **UW** | ~4M metro. Cannot be fed. Full stop | Sprawling, indefensible, water on both sides | Level-one trauma centre; and Seattle **owns its own dams and its own watershed**, which is a categorically different power and water story | **Failure, or conquest.** Triage at civilizational scale |

**Recommendation: the UVic shape.** An island polity of a few hundred thousand, with farmland at hand, a fisheries economy, a real capital's institutional furniture to loot, and a water boundary that makes the perimeter problem tractable. Muddle the name; keep the geography.

## What the region gives you

- **Rain and mild winters.** A near-year-round growing season, no deep-freeze mortality, abundant hydro potential. Costs: relentless fungal crop disease, vitamin D deficiency at scale, and rot as the enemy of every stored thing — grain, paper, leather, film. The Library's fight is against damp, not fire.
- **Salmon and shellfish.** This is the fact that makes the whole setting survivable: high-quality protein at scale with no industrial input, requiring boats, nets, smokehouses and salt. It also means **fisheries are the primary external treaty issue**, which routes directly into the sovereignty question below.
- **Douglas fir.** Fuel, timber, charcoal, and a biomass district-energy supply that regenerates. A forested maritime region is the best possible place to run a pre-petroleum industrial base.
- **Water and boats.** Control of the strait is control of trade. A polity with a shipyard and a naval base is a regional power; a polity with neither is a customer.
- **Hydro is a choice you should make deliberately.** Distant large dams are unholdable — long transmission lines through territory you don't control. Micro-hydro on island creeks is holdable and small. A city that *owns its own dams* (the Seattle case) is a fundamentally different polity from one that used to buy power from a provincial crown corporation.
- **The subduction zone.** A Cascadia megathrust earthquake is sitting there as either your inciting event or your act-two hinge. My advice: don't open with it. Slow decay is the more interesting engine, and a quake landing on a polity that has already deferred maintenance for fifteen years is a far better catastrophe than one landing on a functioning state.
- **The border.** Two federal collapses proceeding at different rates make the 49th parallel a resource rather than a line — arbitrage, refuge, jurisdictional laundering, and a place to put things you don't want inspected. And Cascadian regionalist sentiment gives your polity a pre-written legitimating myth it did not have to invent.

## Indigenous nations are co-sovereigns, not background

This is load-bearing and getting it wrong would be both a craft failure and worse than that.

These campuses sit on Lekwungen and W̱SÁNEĆ territory, on Musqueam land, on Duwamish land. Neighbouring nations enter this scenario with functioning governments, land bases, fishing rights, kinship networks that cross the border, and in many cases better continuity of local ecological knowledge than the university has. They are not a frontier. They are the other state.

Three sharp threads:

- **The paper admission becomes enforceable.** These universities have spent decades formally, publicly, and repeatedly acknowledging that they are on unceded or treaty land. That was a statement with no legal consequence because there was a superior sovereign standing between the words and their implications. Remove the Crown and the acknowledgment is a signed admission of title, entered into the record by the party against whom it will be used, with no court of appeal above the parties. That is an extraordinary premise and I have not seen it used.
- **The Crown is the treaty counterparty — so who inherits the obligation?** On southern Vancouver Island the Douglas Treaties of the 1850s are narrow, contested in their terms, and geographically right on top of the campus; most of the rest of British Columbia is unceded outright. South of the border, Point Elliott and the *Boldt* line guarantee tribal fishing rights *federally* — the enforcer disappears and the right does not, and neither does the tribes' organizational capacity to assert it. In a fishery-dependent economy, that is the central external negotiation of the setting.
- **The museum collections are a bargaining chip**, and everyone at the table knows it.

The realistic outcome is a **negotiated order between co-sovereigns**: fisheries agreements, shared jurisdiction, extradition, credential recognition, students, physicians and reciprocal medical access, and the university's expertise traded for food and passage. Sometimes alliance, sometimes cold war, never a blank space on the map. This is also where the university's universalist self-image gets its hardest test, because a treaty means admitting there is something it cannot simply decide.

# Part IV — Phases, and the frictions that make it a story

## The periodization

**Phase 1 — Substitution (years, and nobody names it).** Grants arrive late, then partial, then unpredictably. Hiring freezes, deferred maintenance, deferred maintenance, deferred maintenance. Meanwhile the university starts quietly doing things the state stopped doing: its clinic sees non-students because the walk-in closed, its shuttle runs the route transit cut, its lab does the water testing the region can no longer do. Every one of these is framed internally as *community engagement*, and it is entirely sincere. **This is where the best fiction is**, because the characters have no idea they are founding a country.

**Phase 2 — Absorption.** Refugee professionals arrive: the municipal engineer, the public health officer, the provincial archivist with three vans of records. The university hires them because it can and because someone should. Payroll shifts partly in kind — housing, meals, heat, tuition for children. And the endowment, which was paper, becomes worthless as paper and enormously valuable as what it was actually secured against: **land.** UBC's holdings are literally called the Endowment Lands. The university discovers it is a landlord with tenants, and a landlord with tenants is a government.

**Phase 3 — The quorum crisis.** The legal rupture described above. A meeting that cannot be held; a memo that says it can. Six months of committee work, one document, no violence, and afterwards a state exists.

**Phase 4 — Consolidation.** A perimeter. Taxation in kind. Courts with real sentences. A currency, or credible ration scrip. The first execution. The first war, which will be about fish or about a valley. This phase is where the polity becomes something its founders would not have voted for.

**Phase 5 — Legitimation.** The myth gets written, and it gets taught. Convocation becomes the citizenship rite. The curriculum becomes the civic religion. A history department writes the official account of phase three and the memo is either canonized or quietly lost. Somebody proposes that a chair should pass to a child.

## The frictions

**Deliberation versus siege.** Faculty governance is slow by design — that slowness is the whole point of it, a defense against a hasty administration. A crop failure does not respect a committee that meets monthly. Every emergency is an argument for executive power and every grant of executive power is permanent. **This is the central political drama of the setting** and it never resolves, which is correct.

**Tenure is a life appointment, which is to say an aristocracy.** It was defensible as a protection for unpopular inquiry. It looks different when the tenured hold allocation power over food and heat, and different again when the second generation asks whether a chair is heritable. The hereditary professorship is the setting's natural corruption arc.

**Students are the internal threat, not the external one.** Twenty thousand transient people who suddenly cannot leave: literate, unpropertied, young, already organized in a students' union with elected officers, a treasury, a newspaper, and a native protest repertoire of occupation and strike. They have every structural grievance and all the institutional tools. Meanwhile **sessionals and adjuncts are a pre-installed underclass** with faculty skills and no faculty security. The class conflict does not need to be invented; it is already in the org chart, and it is between two groups who both teach the same students.

**Credential versus competence.** The Curriculum Committee's licensing monopoly is the university's greatest power and its most brittle. When the licensed physician is worse than the unlicensed midwife with thirty years of practice, the polity has to choose between its legitimacy and its outcomes.

**Openness versus classification.** The institution's deepest instinct is to publish. The state's instinct is to classify. A library that lends to a rival city is either the last redoubt of the university's soul or a treason case, and both readings are available to the same act. Academic freedom is either the first casualty or the last redoubt — decide which, per phase.

**The self-image.** An institution that understands itself as universalist, critical, and open becomes a wall, a ration line and a gallows. The characters who cannot metabolize that are the ones worth writing.

## Anchors

- **Medieval corporate jurisdiction** — *privilegium fori*, the *taxatio*, the dispersions, and **St Scholastica Day 1355**, where a town-and-gown massacre ended in the university gaining authority over the town. Read for the reframe: these powers existed.
- **Lianda — the National Southwestern Associated University, 1937–46.** Peking, Tsinghua and Nankai universities merged and relocated to Kunming, and sustained teaching, research and faculty governance through invasion, bombing, hyperinflation and total state failure, in improvised buildings, for nine years. The best single precedent for institutional continuity under collapse, and probably the least known.
- **University of Sarajevo under siege, 1992–96.** Taught through it. The National and University Library burned in August 1992. Both the persistence and the loss.
- **The Hudson's Bay Company in Rupert's Land, and the Colony of Vancouver Island.** A chartered corporation exercising sovereign government because the chartering power was too far away to do it. Fort Victoria was theirs.
- **Agricultural extension and the Wisconsin Idea.** A university that already believed the countryside was its constituency.
- **Deaccessioning and preservation-triage literature**, plus the brittle-books problem, for the Library sections. The arguments are real and already bitter.

## Confidence notes

- **Solid:** medieval universities' jurisdictional privileges, the dispersions, and the outcome of St Scholastica Day; the bicameral Board/Senate structure with government-appointed Board seats; campus security's non-sworn status and the RCMP-on-campus asymmetry; meal plans as swipe-based entitlement systems; university district energy systems including biomass supply at UBC; the land-grant/extension tradition and the WSU-versus-UW asymmetry; Lianda; the Sarajevo library's destruction in August 1992; HBC governance of Rupert's Land and Fort Victoria's origin; microfilm's longevity advantage over digital media; Seattle City Light's ownership of its own dams and Seattle's ownership of its watershed.
- **Directionally right, verify before it appears as a stated fact:** all population figures; the specific terms and exact geography of the Douglas Treaties relative to campus; the precise holdings of *Boldt* and Point Elliott; the acreage of the UBC farm; the current state of any named energy facility. All fine as written, none safe to quote precisely.
- **My extrapolation, not history:** the five-mechanism taxonomy; the Proctors/Field Force split; the two-census feud between the Registrar and Food Services; the quorum-crisis-as-founding-memo; the prestige inversion; the phase model; and the claim that land acknowledgments become enforceable admissions. All plausible, none attested. The last one is the most speculative thing in the brief and also the most interesting, which is usually how that works.
- **Charming but unsupported, flagged so you don't lean on it:** that Classics becomes the school of government because classicists understand pre-industrial administration. Pleasing, and I can't defend it. The departments that would actually govern are Public Administration, Political Science, Law, History, Nursing, and — unglamorously and genuinely — Library and Archival Studies.
