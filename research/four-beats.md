### Research support for: the seed bank, the circuit, the instruments, the voyage

*Research brief. Confidence notes at the end.*

## The spine: beats one and four are the same beat, a century apart

Before the details, the structural observation, because it's the thing that makes these four beats one story rather than four good scenes.

**The ship sails carrying medicine because a hundred years earlier someone chose seed over supper.**

The founding martyr didn't die for a seed collection. They died establishing a proposition — that a thing held in trust outranks the hunger of the person holding it. A polity that believes that can rate a chronometer over thirty years, plant timber it will not live to cut, send its medical students into provinces that cannot pay, and spend a schooner and twenty lives on a city it has never seen and cannot verify exists. A polity that doesn't believe it eats the seed in year one and is a village by year forty.

So beat four is the founding myth's *audit*. The voyage is the moment the polity discovers whether the thing it has been teaching for four generations is true about itself. Nobody who remembers the long winter is alive to see it, which is exactly why it counts — the ethic has become native rather than remembered.

Say that once, quietly, and never again.

# Beat 1 — The martyr at the seed bank doors

> **Canon correction.** The anniversary is **the vote, not the death**, and the heroism is **thermal, not martial** — he held a key to a room of edible seed and opened it as rarely as possible. The mob is deleted. The multiplier arithmetic below is therefore a *floor speech* to a body that divided nineteen to twenty-three, not a private justification; and the door is a heat leak with a budget of openings, not a barricade. My posthumous-conferral invention needs re-pointing. See [canon-reconciliation.md](canon-reconciliation.md).

## This happened

The single best thing I can tell you: **you have independently reinvented the Vavilov Institute.**

Leningrad, 1941–44. Staff of the All-Union Institute of Plant Industry held the world's largest seed collection through a 900-day siege in which perhaps a million people died of starvation, and several of them starved to death rather than eat it. Alexander Shchukin, the groundnut curator, died at his desk. Dmitri Ivanov died surrounded by thousands of packets of rice. Nikolai Vavilov himself — whose collection it was — starved to death in prison in Saratov in 1943 while his staff starved guarding his work.

Two details make the real event better than the invented one:

- **The collection included living tuber holdings that could not simply be stored.** Potatoes are clonal; they must be replanted annually or the accession dies. So the staff *planted out under shelling*. The martyrdom was not passive custody in a cold room. It was labour — going out to a field, in a siege, to put the collection in the ground so it would still exist next year.
- **The custodians ate around it.** They boiled leather, glue, sawdust. The seed was in the room.

Use the shape, not the specifics. But the fact that it is attested means you can write it without flinching, and it gives you the texture invention won't: the desk, the packets, the field.

## Why the seed and not the food — the arithmetic that makes it defensible

The beat only works if the trade is *actually rational*, otherwise the martyr is a fanatic and the polity is founded on a lunacy. It is rational, and the argument should be quotable in-world:

A seed potato eaten is one meal for one person. Planted, it returns something on the order of five to fifteen times its weight in one season, plus next year's seed. A packet of wheat is a mouthful; sown, it's a field. **The multiplier is the moral argument.** Eating seed converts a permanent asset into a single afternoon, and a polity that does it once has to do it every year forever, from a smaller base each time.

The counter-argument has to be voiced by someone sympathetic, because it's strong: the multiplier only pays if you survive to the harvest, and the person starving in front of you will not. There is no clean answer. Don't give one.

## Germplasm regeneration — the technical fact that makes the beat make sense

**A seed bank is not a vault. It's a farm on a rotation.** This is the detail that makes the collection fragile enough to be worth dying for.

- Seed viability is species-specific and shorter than people assume. Alliums — onion, leek — are effectively dead in a year or two. Brassicas last a few years. Wheat, barley, tomato will hold for decades if genuinely cool and dry. Recalcitrant seeds like oak and chestnut cannot be dried at all and must be kept alive or kept growing.
- So every accession has a clock, and the bank must **grow out** each one before its clock runs down: plant it, isolate it from cross-pollination, harvest it, clean it, dry it, and return fresh seed to store. Miss a rotation and that accession is gone, permanently, with no way to know until someone sows it years later and nothing comes up.
- **Potatoes and other clonal crops have no seed option at all.** Replant every year or lose the cultivar.

Now put that in Cascadia, which is close to the worst climate on earth for it: **damp.** Seed dies of moisture faster than anything else. The bank needs desiccant — silica gel regenerated over a fire, quicklime, wood ash, charcoal — inside sealed glass or waxed tins, held cold, in a region where everything wants to mould. Absorption refrigeration if they have it (see the penicillin brief); an ice house and a dry cellar if they don't.

Which gives you the reason the death is **at the doors**: the bank is not a bunker. It's a working building with a grow-out field, a drying room, a fire for the desiccant, and a door that people come to. The martyr died at a threshold they were keeping, on the way in or out of ordinary work.

## Why a doctoral candidate specifically — and the posthumous convocation

The institutional logic is good and you should keep it: by the long winter the senior staff have died, walked out, or been requisitioned into feeding the city. The person left holding the collection is a junior — a candidate who was there in the first place because their thesis was there. Their research question has become absurd. Their custody has become absolute. **The lowest-status full member of the institution holds the line**, which is the correct shape for a founding myth about an institution.

And then the invention that ties this brief to the other two:

**They die uncredentialed.** The degree was never conferred. So the new state's first sovereign act — one of the first things it does after the quorum crisis, possibly in the same session — is to confer a degree on a corpse.

That is why convocation becomes the citizenship rite. Not because someone designed it that way, but because the polity's founding legal act was a posthumous conferral, and every convocation since has been a re-enactment of it. The thing that makes you a citizen is the thing they did to a dead person in year three. You now have a causal chain running from the seed bank through the Registrar's office to the ration card.

## How the myth is built, and how it goes wrong

Expect all of this, and expect the state to be self-conscious about some of it:

- **The fast day.** One day a year, no ration issued, everyone hungry on purpose. Cheap, universal, and it does the ideological work of the multiplier argument without anyone having to make it.
- **The doors as relic.** Not replaced. Not repaired. Marked. Anyone can go and look, which is much more powerful than a shrine nobody can enter.
- **A line from the notebook on the seal.** The martyr's actual working notes — accession numbers, moisture readings, a grow-out schedule — become scripture, and the most quoted line is probably something clerical and flat. That's better than eloquence.
- **The oath on the ration card.** Everyone who draws food affirms the proposition. Which means everyone who has ever stolen from the store has broken an oath, which is how you get a crime that is also a heresy.

And the revisionism, which is a whole arc for the later era:

- A historian finds the door was **locked from outside**. Or that the martyr was delirious by then and the choice wasn't a choice. Or that the collection was already dead — the desiccant fire had gone out weeks before and every accession in the room was mould.
- Or worse and more human: that someone *else* ate first, and was quietly written out.
- **The state's response to the revision tells you what kind of state it is by year 100.** Suppression, absorption ("the myth is true in the way that matters"), or a genuinely brave publication. Any of the three is a scene, and the third one is the most interesting because it's the university's original instinct reasserting itself against its own state.

# Beat 2 — The circuit

## The template is the assize, not the clinic

Modern medical education already contains this beat: clerkships, preceptorships, rural rotations, logbooks, sign-offs, an examiner. Generalizing it costs nothing. What makes it *statecraft* is the older template — **the circuit court**, the assize, the itinerant justice. The capital's authority arrives periodically, in person, along a fixed route, and for the days it's there, it *is* the state.

Historical anchors worth reading: China's **barefoot doctors** (1965–81) — short training, rural deployment, enormous measurable gains in basic outcomes; the Russian and Soviet **feldsher**, a mid-level practitioner who was the only medicine most villages saw; Cuban rural service years; and pre-Flexner apprenticeship medicine, which is what your riders' training actually resembles.

## The circuit does five jobs at once

Only the first is what it says on the tin:

1. **Education.** You are not a physician until you have ridden. The circuit year is the rite of passage and the thing that makes the cohort a cohort.
2. **Care delivery.** The province's only medicine.
3. **The census.** Births, deaths and causes, pregnancies, vaccinations given.
4. **Intelligence.** Crop condition, food stores, who's armed, who's angry, who's talking to whom. Nobody calls it intelligence.
5. **The face of the capital.** For most people in the province, the rider is the only representative of the state they will ever meet, and their opinion of the polity is an opinion about a specific twenty-four-year-old.

**Job three is worth more to the state than job two.** The treatments save individuals; the ledger is how the capital knows anything at all about its own territory. Which means the ledger is the thing the rider must not lose, and losing it is a career-ending event out of proportion to any clinical error.

## The kit, because the beat lives in the pack

Weight is the constraint — one horse, or one boat. What's in it:

- **Drugs.** Penicillin V tablets in sealed tins, which is *why this beat is possible at all* — acid-stable, oral, no cold chain, no vials, no needles (see the penicillin brief). Sulfa as the workhorse. Aspirin. Morphine, controlled and counted, signed for at both ends. Atropine. Ergot for postpartum haemorrhage — dangerous and indispensable. Digoxin, narrow margin. Quinine if trade supplies it.
- **Instruments.** Scalpel and blades, artery forceps, needles with silk or catgut, a syringe, a tourniquet, splints, and **tooth forceps** — dentistry will be a third of the work and nobody writes about it.
- **Sterilizing.** A small pressure vessel, or a spirit lamp and a boiling pan and the discipline to use it.
- **Diagnostics.** Stethoscope. A glass thermometer, precious and breakable, and the loss of it mid-circuit is a real crisis.
- **The vaccine, carried in people.** Smallpox by arm-to-arm transfer means the rider maintains a living chain of vaccinees along the route — the immunity is the cargo, and breaking the chain ends the round. That's a wonderful concrete problem: you cannot stop for a week, because the chain has a schedule.
- **The ledger, the seal, and the drug ledger's counterfoil.**

## What the rider faces, and cannot do

The province dies of: obstructed labour and puerperal sepsis, tetanus after a farm injury, childhood diarrhoeal disease, tuberculosis, pneumonia, trauma from logging and boats and animals, dental abscess, syphilis, and hunger.

The drama is in the absences. No imaging. No blood bank in the field. No anaesthetist — the rider gives the ether and does the surgery, or a villager gives the ether. No second opinion for eleven days. Appendicitis on day four of an eight-day leg, and the choice between operating badly and moving the patient, either of which may kill them. A caesarean by someone who has seen three.

And the thing that will actually break a character: the rider knows, precisely, what the capital's hospital could do for this patient, and knows the distance.

## Failure modes — pick two, they're all good

- **Going native.** The village offers respect, a house, a place, work that visibly matters — everything the capital's hierarchy denied them. Riders who don't come back are a recurring administrative problem and, from the province's side, a recruitment strategy.
- **The rider as vector.** An itinerant practitioner moving between settlements on a schedule is an epidemiological delivery system. Historically this is exactly what happened. The circuit spreads what it treats.
- **The resistant strain arrives in the ledger before it arrives in person.** The rider writes down four cases that didn't respond, and the capital reads it three weeks later, and the clock from the penicillin brief starts ticking audibly.
- **The counterfeit rider.** A stolen bag, a plausible manner, chalk in the tins. Which means credentials have to be verifiable *by people who cannot read* — a seal, a token, an instrument pattern only the university issues, a call-and-response. Authentication again.
- **Extortion**, and its mirror: villages that hoard a patient to guarantee the next visit.

## The politics

The circuit *is* the hinterland treaty in operation. The province sends grain and children; the capital sends riders. So the **schedule is a political document** — which valleys get a rider in what month, who gets the winter gap, whose village is a full stop and whose is a half-day. A province shorted on the rota has a genuine grievance and a genuine lever, and the lever is planting something other than maize.

Internally: a returning cohort is a faction. They have shared trauma, cross-village loyalties, and a well-founded conviction that the senior faculty who set the drug allocations have not seen a province in twenty years.

# Beat 3 — The instruments, the timber, the voyage

## The thesis of this beat

**A polity that can make an instrument is more secure than one that owns a better instrument it cannot repair.**

That is the answer to "nobody from the apocalypse is alive anymore," and it's why this beat is hopeful rather than elegiac. By year 100 the salvage era is over — rubber has perished, the last pre-collapse bearings are worn out, the tubes have gassed, the steel has rusted. Nothing on that ship was found. **Everything on that ship was made.** The polity is poorer in absolute terms than the one that collapsed and infinitely more secure, because it understands every object it depends on.

## Chronometers, and why the observatory is a ministry

**Why it matters, in one calculation.** Latitude is easy — the sun at noon, a sextant, ten minutes of arithmetic. Longitude requires knowing the time at a reference meridian, and the arithmetic is brutal: four seconds of clock error is one nautical mile of longitude at the equator. One minute of error is fifteen miles. A chronometer losing two seconds a day over a thirty-day passage puts you fifteen miles wrong, which off an unlit rock coast is the difference between a landfall and a wreck.

**The insight that makes this a state function:** you do not need a clock that is accurate. You need a clock whose error is *known and constant*. A chronometer that loses 2.3 seconds a day, every day, reliably, is a perfectly good chronometer — you correct for it. So the object Engineering delivers is not a clock, it's **a clock plus a rate certificate**, and the certificate is worth more than the mechanism.

Which means a chronometer cannot be made by Engineering alone. Establishing a rate requires **astronomical time**: a transit instrument on a meridian, a pendulum regulator in a temperature-stable cellar, a run of star transits over weeks, and a comparison log. That's an observatory, and it's a permanent institution with a permanent staff, and it exists to serve navigation and surveying.

**So time joins potency and mass as the polity's third exported standard.** The noon gun or the time ball is a daily public assertion of sovereignty. A visiting captain who wants their chronometer rated pays for it, in a currency the observatory sets. Weights, measures, potency, time — the polity's real export is *certainty*, which is exactly what a university would end up selling.

Mechanically, for texture: a temperature-compensated balance (bimetallic, or a nickel-steel alloy if their metallurgy is good), a detent escapement, jeweled bearings — and synthetic corundum has been industrially makeable since the early 1900s by flame fusion, so the polity can grow its own rubies. Chronometers are stored gimballed, wound at the same hour daily by the same person, and never allowed to stop. Three of them, because two that disagree tell you nothing.

## Sextants, and the instrument that makes instruments

A sextant is an arc, two mirrors, shades, and a telescope. The mirrors and the telescope are glass-shop work. The hard part is **the divided arc** — engraving a scale accurate to fractions of a minute of arc — and that requires a **dividing engine**, a precision machine whose whole purpose is making other instruments accurate.

The dividing engine is the treasure. It's the tool that lets a machine shop produce *repeatable precision* rather than one good piece by one gifted hand, and a university machine shop can build one. If you want a single object to stand for the polity's technical sovereignty, it's not the chronometer — it's the engine in the basement that graduates the arcs, and the fact that the polity built it rather than found it.

Every sextant also has an **index error** — a small permanent offset, measured and written on the instrument, checked against the horizon or a star. Same principle as the rate certificate: the error is not a defect, it's a documented property. That's a whole worldview and it's the university's worldview.

## The almanac office — where the computer scientists went

**You cannot use a sextant without ephemerides.** Predicted positions of sun, moon, planets and stars for every day, tabulated in advance. Inherited almanacs run out — and worse, they were computed for a specific epoch, so as the years pass the star positions drift with precession and the old tables quietly stop being right.

So the polity computes its own. That requires orbital theory, and it requires **computers**, in the original sense: people who compute. Rooms of them, on shifts, with logarithm tables and mechanical calculators and a checking regime where two independent computers must agree before a figure enters the almanac.

**This is where the discipline of computer science went after the machines died** — the department that was the most modern in year −25 becomes the most archaic by year 60, and its members know it, and they are the only people in the polity trained to think in algorithms, error propagation and verification. The almanac is published annually, a year or two in advance, and it is the single most impressive artifact the state produces: a book of the future, printed on the press, correct.

If you want a chronometer-independent fallback: **lunar distances.** It works, it needs no clock, and the arithmetic is so miserable that it's the thing the almanac office is proudest of.

Charts: inherited, and increasingly wrong. Depths shift, aids to navigation are gone, lighthouses dark. A pilot's memory is a state asset and pilots are licensed, which is the Curriculum Committee again.

## Forestry — the emotional center

**Forestry plants trees for hulls that will be cut in eighty years.**

That is the fact to build the beat on. A polity maintaining a silvicultural rotation is a polity that has decided, institutionally and in writing, that it will exist in 2150 — and it has committed labour to that belief, annually, in a ledger, for people none of them will meet. Nobody who remembers the apocalypse is alive, and the forestry office is planning for the great-grandchildren of people who aren't born. That does more work for your era than the entire navigation section.

The technical substance:

- **Ship timber means specific shapes, not volume.** Frames and knees need curved grain — compass timber — which you cannot saw out of a straight log. You find it in root crooks and stem forks, or you *grow* it: trees deliberately managed for bends. Which means the forestry office keeps a register of individual standing trees reserved for the shipyard, some of them older than the polity. A knee tree with a number painted on it is a beautiful object.
- **The species.** Douglas fir for spars and heavy structure — Pacific Northwest fir was a serious spar export in the nineteenth century, so the region's reputation is real. Western red cedar for boats and small craft: light, rot-resistant, and the historical Northwest Coast canoe timber. Garry oak for hard shapes, which is scarce and ecologically precious, and the fight over cutting it is a live one.
- **The forest is also the chemical works.** Tar and pitch from destructive distillation of resinous stumps in kilns. Turpentine. Charcoal for the forge. Bark for tanning, and tanned leather is pump seals, bellows, and harness — so the pharmacy's fermenters and the ship's rigging both come out of the same woodlot.
- **Cordage and canvas.** Hemp or flax, a **ropewalk** — a shed a quarter-mile long, which is one of the most distinctive buildings any pre-industrial state builds — and a sail loft. Standing rigging tarred, and the tar comes from the kilns.

## The ship

Not a frigate. A **topsail schooner or ketch, twenty to thirty metres**: shallow enough for uncharted water, rigged so a dozen hands can work it, cheap enough to risk. The historical analogues are the coastal lumber schooners and the Baltic traders — working vessels, sailed by small crews, into bad water, for money.

Two hard constraints:

- **Teredo.** Shipworm is a genuine and serious Pacific coast problem; an unprotected hull in salt water gets eaten from inside. The answer is copper sheathing, and the second-order lesson is that **iron fastenings under copper sheathing destroy themselves galvanically** — so fastenings below the waterline must be copper alloy. The voyage therefore depends on the copper supply, which means salvage or a mine, which means the voyage has a metallurgical prerequisite nobody in the story will find romantic.
- **Sail, not engine.** An auxiliary is possible — producer gas, wood gas, steam — but fuel you can't replace mid-ocean is a liability, and sail is the reliable answer. If they carry an auxiliary it's for harbour work and for the last hour.

**The cargo:** penicillin V in sealed tins, because it survives the passage without cold — the whole voyage is downstream of one chemical fact. Sulfa. Vaccine, which is the cold problem, in an ice chest or as dried lymph. Antitoxin if they dare. Instruments. And two people who matter more than the cargo: **a physician and a rider**, because a drug without a practitioner is a tin of powder. Plus the assay standard, for the reason below.

## The radio — and the two-way verification problem

**Receive is cheap. Transmit is expensive.** This asymmetry is the best thing about the radio thread.

A crystal set — a coil, a capacitor, a galena or pyrite detector, headphones — needs no power whatsoever, and HF skywave propagation needs no infrastructure between the two parties, only the ionosphere. So the polity can *hear* the world with almost nothing. Answering it requires a real transmitter: power, a mast, tubes or equivalents that are genuinely hard to manufacture consistently, and an antenna system. **The polity has been listening for decades and can barely reply.**

Which gives you the scene: the distress call sits in the log for two days while they get the transmitter up.

Propagation details worth having, because they're strange and true:

- **The polity's radio horizon is discontinuous.** Skywave means you hear a city two thousand kilometres away better than a town a hundred kilometres away, which is inside the skip zone. You know the far city and not the near valley.
- **It's seasonal and it follows the solar cycle.** Some years a band is open nightly; some years it's dead. **A solar minimum can silence a diplomatic relationship for years**, and the polity's astronomers can predict it, badly, which makes them politically important.
- **CW, not voice.** Morse gets through on a fraction of the power. The distress call arrives as hand-keyed code, with a fist a practiced operator can recognize as a person.
- **Scheduled nets.** Cities keep a listening watch at agreed hours on agreed frequencies. So the call is heard *because a watchkeeper was at the set at 0200, as they are every night, as their predecessor was.* That watchkeeper is a character, and their job is nearly always nothing.

**And then the verification problem, in both directions, which is the through-line of all three briefs:**

- **Inbound: the call cannot be confirmed without sailing.** It might be a city that no longer exists, transmitting on a schedule nobody is left to cancel. It might be an automatic keyer running on a hydro trickle in an empty room. It might be a trap. It might be an epidemic that will burn out before a schooner can cross — and the voyage is *longer than the epidemic*, which is the tragedy built into the beat. They may arrive to a city that has finished dying, or finished recovering, and either way they arrive too late to have been necessary and too late to turn back.
- **Outbound: the receiving city has no way to know the medicine is real.** Counterfeit antibiotic is historically attested and would be endemic in this world. So the ship carries the assay standard and someone who can demonstrate potency on a plate in front of witnesses — a public assay in a foreign harbour, which is one of the best scenes available in the whole setting.

**Authentication is what this polity actually exports.** Potency, weights, time, credentials, and a callsign. The medicine is almost incidental; what the far city is really being offered is *a party whose claims can be checked*. That is what the university became.

## Distances, if you want to fix the voyage's length

From southern Vancouver Island: San Francisco is roughly 700 nautical miles — call it seven to twelve days under working sail. Southeast Alaska, similar. Hawaii is around 2,300 nautical miles, so three weeks out and considerably more back against the wind. Asia is a season.

Pick by how long you want them gone and by whether you want them to arrive too late.

## Confidence notes

- **Solid:** the Vavilov Institute siege story including Shchukin and Ivanov, and Vavilov's own death by starvation in prison in 1943; seed viability being species-specific and short for alliums; clonal crops requiring annual replanting; the four-seconds-per-nautical-mile longitude relation; the rate certificate and index error as the actual basis of instrument use; dividing engines as the enabling tool for graduated instruments; synthetic corundum being industrially producible since the early twentieth century; nautical almanacs requiring human computers; teredo as a serious Pacific coast hull threat; galvanic corrosion driving copper-alloy fastenings under copper sheathing; western red cedar as the Northwest Coast small-craft timber; crystal sets requiring no power; HF skywave propagation, the skip zone, and solar-cycle dependence; CW's power advantage over voice; barefoot doctors and feldshers.
- **Directionally right, verify before it lands as a stated fact:** the number who died guarding the Vavilov collection (usually given as around nine, sources vary); potato multiplication ratios; the "premier worldwide" framing for Douglas fir spars — the export trade was real and significant, the superlative I'd check; all the passage distances and durations; specific viability figures for any named species.
- **My extrapolation, not history:** the posthumous convocation and its causal link to the citizenship rite; the fast day and the ration-card oath; the almanac office as the successor to computer science; time as a formally exported standard alongside potency and mass; the registered knee tree; the two-day delay while the transmitter comes up; and the reading that the polity's real export is authentication. All plausible, none attested.
- **The one I'd handle carefully:** vaccination by arm-to-arm chain is real historical practice and it works dramatically, but it also transmitted other diseases, which is why it was abandoned. If the rider is maintaining a living chain, a competent medical service in year 60 knows the risk and has decided to accept it — make that a decision someone signed, not an oversight.
