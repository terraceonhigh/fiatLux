# Cropping a Summer-Dry Coast

### Market gardening, seed, and soil on a maritime strip that stops raining in July

*Research memo, companion in register to `research/water-systems-under-failure.md`. Real-world facts only — not tied to any fictional setting's numbers. Geography throughout is the Pacific Northwest coastal strip (Willamette Valley through Puget Sound to southern Vancouver Island), Köppen Csb/Cfb: wet October–May, near-rainless July–August.*

---

## I. The dryland calendar is real, and it is not the shape you might expect

The intuitive model — a Mediterranean year of fall-sown crops harvested by midsummer, then bare ground until the October rains — is half right, and the half that is wrong is the more interesting half.

**The fall-sown calendar exists and works.** Winter cereals (wheat, barley) go in from late September through October west of the Cascades and come off in July–August, having done nearly all their growing on winter and spring rain. Fava beans sown October to mid-November overwinter in the mild maritime regime and harvest June–July. Garlic planted in October is lifted in July and is, in practice, drought-proof: its entire bulbing cycle is finished before the deficit bites. Overwintering onions sown in August bulb in June. Autumn-established brassicas — purple sprouting broccoli is the canonical one, transplanted mid-to-late August, hardy to roughly 10 °F / −12 °C — stand through the winter and crop from late February into May, filling exactly the months when nothing else is fresh. And the hay premise holds: cool-season grass growth peaks in spring, dryland pasture goes summer-dormant, and the one reliable cut comes in May–June.

**But the documented dry-farming practice of this exact region is the other calendar: spring-planted summer crops grown to harvest on stored winter moisture.** Oregon State University's Dry Farming Program and the Dry Farming Collaborative (roughly 30 trial farms in the Willamette Valley at its height) grew tomatoes, potatoes, winter squash, zucchini, melons, flour and flint corn, and dry beans **with zero irrigation** in a valley receiving about 40 inches (~1,000 mm) of rain a year of which often less than 2 inches falls in July and August combined. The method: plant early into remaining soil moisture, at wide spacing and low density; keep a loose shallow-cultivated surface ("dust mulch") to break capillary rise; choose varieties selected for the practice (dry-farmed Early Girl tomatoes are the standard example). OSU's working definition of dry farming requires **at least 20 inches (~500 mm) of annual rainfall** falling in the wet season.

Two conditions gate that second calendar, and both matter here.

**First, it is soil-mapped, not uniform.** Spring-planted dry farming works on deep soils with high water-holding capacity, where winter rain is banked a metre or more down and roots can follow it. Shallow, gravelly, or sandy ground cannot bank the water, and no management makes it do so. On a farm whose worst ground is a recently sand-capped playing field (Section IV), the Mediterranean model is correct *for that ground* — fall-sown, off by midsummer — while the deep ground can carry tomatoes, squash, corn and beans clear through the drought. The dryland calendar is a property of the soil map, not of the farm.

**Second, the rainfall floor is not uniformly met across the named coast.** The Olympic rain shadow puts Victoria at roughly 640 mm/yr and Sequim at roughly 400–460 mm, against 1,200–1,560 mm at Vancouver stations — a three-to-fourfold range across a strait. "Stored winter rainfall" is a real bank account everywhere on this coast, but its opening balance differs by location enough to decide whether the spring-planted calendar is viable at all.

One honest contradiction to note: the overwintering brassicas that anchor the fall-sown calendar are **established in August**, the driest weeks of the year. With no summer water at all, establishment waits for the September rains and the plants go into winter smaller, which is survivable (smaller plants often overwinter better) but narrows the window. A "no summer water" calendar still has a small water bill in late August, or it pays in transplant size instead.

And the ground is not bare after midsummer harvest — it stands in stubble or dust-mulched fallow until the rains return, then goes immediately to cover crops or the next fall sowing. Bare October ground on this coast is a nutrient leak: the winter rain that recharges the soil also strips its nitrate.

## II. Where the missed weeks land decides whether the year is lost

The premise — that timing beats depth, and that the yield-determining window matters more than the peak-deficit date — is the standard finding, and it is worth having with its source. The framework is Doorenbos & Kassam, *Yield Response to Water* (FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 33, 1979): relative yield loss equals a crop- and stage-specific factor **Ky** times the relative evapotranspiration deficit. Seasonal Ky runs 1.25 for maize, 1.10 for potato, 1.15 for dry beans — meaning a season-long 20% water shortfall costs those crops *more* than 20% of yield. But the stage-wise tables are the point: **sensitivity concentrates at flowering and yield formation** — silking and pollination in corn, flowering and pod set in beans, tuber initiation and early bulking in potato, grain fill in cereals — with the vegetative and ripening stages far more forgiving. The stage-wise figures commonly reproduced for maize put flowering-stage Ky around 1.5 against roughly 0.4 in the vegetative stage: the same missing water, several times the damage.

So the premise is confirmed in its core, with three complications that change how a one-to-three-week outage actually plays:

**The worst single timing is not always mid-season — it can be week one.** A transplant that loses water in its first two weeks dies outright; there is no Ky arithmetic, just a dead planting. The distinction that matters is whether the sowing window is still open. A June transplant loss is replanted and costs a few weeks; the same loss in late July, after the window for that crop has closed, costs the whole planting. Mid-season stress at flowering costs a *fraction* of yield irrecoverably; establishment failure costs *all* of it, recoverably or not depending on the calendar.

**For the market-garden crops themselves, the damage is often quality, not tonnage.** Lettuce bolts and turns bitter; brassicas get tipburn; tomatoes get blossom-end rot under erratic water and split when rain or restored irrigation follows deficit; radishes and turnips go pithy and hot. A leafy crop that survives a dry fortnight can still be unsaleable. The Ky framework, built for field crops, understates this.

**Late failure is confirmed as the mildest — and for some crops it is free, or better than free.** Cereals and dry beans in late fill lose seed size, not the crop, and a truly failing cereal can be cut for hay — the salvage-harvest premise holds. Potatoes stop bulking but hold in the ground. Storage onions and garlic *want* a dry finish. Dry-farmed tomatoes trade yield for concentration, which is why the practice survives commercially. And seed crops (Section III) positively require a rainless ripening. On this coast the late-season drought is not the enemy; it is the reason the region's most valuable crop — seed — is grown here at all.

## III. Seed is the crop a drought hurts twice

The reframe first, because it is the strongest fact in this memo: **the maritime Northwest is not a marginal place to grow seed — it is one of the world's principal biennial seed regions.** Skagit and Snohomish counties in Washington are consistently reported to produce on the order of **75% of US spinach and cabbage seed and ~95% of US table beet seed**, and roughly 8% of world spinach seed, 25% of world cabbage seed, and 25% of world beet seed. The reasons are exactly this climate: winters mild enough that biennials vernalize *in the field* without winter-killing, and summers dry enough that seed ripens clean, without the seed-borne fungal and bacterial disease that wet-summer regions battle. The region's growers coordinate isolation on shared "pinning maps" — an annual pinning day at the WSU research station allocates who grows which crop where — and that isolation requirement is what caps the county's seed acreage at under about 5,000 acres. A small farm taking on the seed role for a district is doing, at miniature scale, what this coast's agriculture actually does.

**The genuinely hard seed crops, and why:**

- **Biennials must live twice.** Carrot, beet, chard, the whole cabbage tribe, onion, leek, parsnip, celery, turnip and rutabaga make no seed in year one. The plants (or lifted-and-replanted roots — "stecklings") occupy ground for 12–14+ months, overwinter, vernalize, and seed the following summer. Every biennial seed crop is a two-season commitment before the first seed exists.
- **Cross-pollinators need distance or discipline.** Insect-pollinated outbreeders (brassicas, carrots, onions) carry commonly cited isolation guidance from several hundred feet to half a mile or more between varieties, and commercial purity standards demand more. Wind-pollinated crops are worse: beet and chard cross each other (and sugar beet) over miles; corn crosses over very long distances. At single-farm scale the practical answer is temporal and varietal, not spatial: **one variety of each cross-pollinating species flowering per year**, which quietly forces the whole district onto a shared varietal calendar. Carrot adds a hazard no map fixes: it crosses freely with wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace), naturalized and ubiquitous on this coast's roadsides, so carrot seed demands aggressive roguing of the surrounding ground.
- **Outbreeders need numbers.** Self-pollinators — beans, peas, lettuce, tomato — hold their quality from a handful of plants, which is why they are every seed-saving culture's entry point. Outbreeders suffer inbreeding depression from small populations: commonly cited working minimums run from a few dozen plants for brassicas, carrots and beets to **100–200+ for corn**, with larger populations always better for maintenance. A seed program's land and labour cost is set by these numbers, not by how much seed is wanted.
- **Some seed simply dies young.** Onion, leek and parsnip seed is reliably good for only **about 1–2 years**; carrot and pea roughly 3; brassicas, tomato and lettuce 4–6+ under cool dry storage. The short-lived group must be regenerated on a nearly continuous cycle because no seed bank can buffer them.

**What a drought interruption costs a seed crop that it does not cost a food crop.** Four distinct things, and they compound:

1. **Exposure.** Much of the food harvest precedes peak drought; seed maturity does not. A carrot is eaten in June; carrot seed is not ripe until August of the following year. The seed crop must stand through the failure window *by definition of being a seed crop*.
2. **The damage propagates.** Maternal water stress during flowering and seed fill reduces not just seed number but seed *quality* — germination percentage, seedling vigor, seed size — and the effect is documented to carry into the next generation's performance even when that generation is well-watered (shown experimentally in soybean; reviewed across food crops). A drought-stressed seed crop can look successfully harvested, weigh out respectably, and still fail in the seed tray the following spring. **A food crop failure costs its calories; a seed crop failure costs the ability to plant.**
3. **A partial failure narrows the population.** If a dry fortnight at flowering means only a fraction of an outbreeding population sets seed, the harvest passes through a genetic bottleneck. The drought does not have to kill a variety to degrade it; three stressed seasons of a 200-plant corn population saved from the best 30 survivors is inbreeding depression on an instalment plan.
4. **The time signature is different.** A failed annual food crop costs one season. A biennial seed crop lost in its second summer costs the two seasons already invested, plus however long until the next cohort seeds — and a farm that wants seed *every* year must run overlapping cohorts, so one lost year-two crop tears a hole in a pipeline, not a pantry.

**The named list where failure costs two seasons rather than one** is the biennial list: carrot, beet and chard, cabbage-tribe brassicas, onion, leek, parsnip, celery, turnip/rutabaga. Worst in class: **onion, leek and parsnip**, which are biennial *and* short-lived of seed — the two-season loss with no seed-bank cushion behind it. Carrot is next, adding the wild-carrot contamination risk; beet and chard add the wind-pollination isolation burden. By contrast the crops a settlement learns to keep seed for first — beans, peas, lettuce, tomato — are annual, self-pollinating, and reasonably long-lived in storage: forgiving on every axis at once.

## IV. Making arable ground out of a playing field

> **Retired as a plot point on 2026-08-11.** Terrace cut the repurposed-playing-fields material; the farm's expansion is now glass and open field. This section is kept because the agronomy is sound and may serve elsewhere, but nothing in the chapter should depend on it. See `notes/spitball.md`, *What the farm actually is*.

What amenity turf actually is, agronomically: **compacted by design and traffic, with its organic matter concentrated in the top few centimetres** as thatch and root mat, over a subsoil that has often not been loosened in decades. Bulk density is high, infiltration is poor, and rooting depth — the whole basis of Section I's stored-moisture calendar — is shallow until the compaction is physically broken.

**Sand-capped fields are a separate and worse case.** Sports-turf construction deliberately caps the ground with a deep sand rootzone *because* sand sheds water and resists compaction; the standard specification is around 30 cm of conforming sand. Sand holds roughly 0.05–0.10 cm of plant-available water per cm of depth against roughly 0.15–0.20 for loams, and has a fraction of the nutrient-holding capacity (cation exchange). **The sand cap is a decision the ground remembers.** Fertility can be composted into it season by season; water-holding capacity at depth cannot be, on any timescale shorter than decades of heavy organic-matter import. The realistic disposition is to treat sand-capped ground as the *last* to dry-farm and the first claim on whatever irrigation or stored water exists — or to hold it in fall-sown crops and early potatoes that finish before the deficit, per Section I.

**Breaking the sod itself is the easy part, with two known traps.** Decomposing turf releases a genuine first-season nitrogen flush, so the first crop off broken sod often flatters the ground. But grassland carries wireworms (click beetle larvae) and leatherjackets (cranefly larvae), which persist in the soil for years after the grass is gone — extension guidance is consistent that root and tuber crops (potatoes, carrots) should not follow newly broken sod, because the larvae tunnel them into unsaleability. The traditional sequence is a cereal, dry beans, or a smother crop for the first year or two, roots later.

**With no purchased inputs, nitrogen is the budget line that binds.** The Pacific Northwest extension figures for overwintered legume cover crops run **roughly 30–150 lb N/acre fixed** (hairy vetch 33–145; Austrian winter pea ~53–100; crimson clover 19–114; fava 25–105), of which very roughly half becomes available to the following crop — OSU's PNW 636 gives the estimation method. That is enough, cycled annually, to run vegetables; it is not enough to also rebuild a degraded soil quickly. Manure is the accelerant, and it matters that in the food-system geometry assumed here, **hay and feed arriving from outlying districts are a nutrient import**: the livestock that eat them are a pipeline moving fertility from the outer rings to the inner one, exactly as classical mixed farming used them. Phosphorus and potassium do not come from legumes at all — they come from manure, urine, and wood ash, and potassium leaches badly on sand.

**Timescale and rotation.** As a synthesis rather than a sourced figure: nitrogen fertility adequate for a respectable vegetable crop is achievable in **one to two seasons** (a full-season legume cover plus manure); soil structure and workability in **two to four**; meaningful gains in deep water-holding capacity are **slower than that everywhere and effectively out of reach on the sand cap**. The rotation that delivers this with abundant labour and no inputs is the classical ley: a third to a half of the ground down to grass-clover at any time, cut or grazed, one to three years per cycle, with vegetables and seed crops taking the freshly broken, fertility-loaded ground and cereals or beans taking the sod-break year. A widely repeated figure for how much water each percentage point of soil organic matter stores per acre circulates in regenerative-agriculture material; it varies by a factor of several between tellings and I could not tie it to a defensible primary source, so it is deliberately not repeated here. The qualitative claim — organic matter raises plant-available water, slowly, in proportion to sustained inputs — is not in dispute.

## V. The portfolio that optimises for the worst fortnight

The organising principle: a farm facing intermittent water failure is not maximising expected yield, it is **maximising the fraction of the year's value that is indifferent to any given one-to-three-week outage.** The practices, with their real costs:

**The missing item first: stored winter water.** Six hundred to sixteen hundred millimetres of rain falls on this coast between October and May, and a pond or cistern filled by it is the oldest irrigation technology there is — gravity-fed, buildable with abundant unskilled labour, lined with puddled clay where the subsoil allows. The arithmetic is friendly at garden scale even if it is hopeless at field scale (the companion memo's 50 t/ha/day figure): each 100 m² of roof under 1,000 mm of annual rain sheds about 100 m³ a year, and Section II says a small volume applied *at the critical stage* buys disproportionate yield — deficit irrigation, in FAO 33's terms, is precisely the science of spending scarce water on the flowering window and nowhere else. Costs: land taken out of production, large volumes of digging, evaporation off open water, and the maintenance discipline the companion memo says is always the first thing to lapse.

**Dry-farm agronomy on the deep ground.** Early planting into moisture, wide spacing and low density, dust-mulch cultivation, deep-set transplants, adapted varieties (the Dry Farming Collaborative's trials exist to identify them; dry-farmed Early Girl is the proof of concept). Cost: substantially lower yield per unit area — OSU is explicit that dry farming is not a yield-maximisation strategy, and the penalty is real but not honestly reducible to one number — plus more area cultivated and weeded per unit of food.

**Staggered sowings and species spread.** Succession plantings mean no single outage catches every cohort at its sensitive stage; species diversity means no single week is critical for everything. Costs: more seed — in direct tension with Section III's seed economy — more labour, and a lower-yielding average because some cohorts always sit badly in the calendar.

**Mulch, with local caveats.** Organic mulch conserves soil moisture and moderates temperature, and the material exists (straw and spoiled hay from the inbound feed trade). The coast-specific costs: it is heaven for slugs, which are this region's signature pest, and it cools soil that heat-loving crops need warm. Mulch suits the perennials and brassicas better than the tomatoes.

**Drip, and what drip is made of.** Drip's case is in the companion memo: 90–95% application efficiency against 70–85% for sprinklers, and it keeps working at pressures where sprinklers have already failed. The caveat belongs in this one: **drip tape and emitters are extruded-polymer industrial consumables with service lives measured in seasons — the irrigation analogue of the companion memo's UV lamp.** A stockpile depletes on a schedule regardless of care. The pre-industrial substitute is buried unglazed clay — olla or pitcher irrigation, documented and effective — which trades the supply chain for potters and heavy labour, and suits beds rather than fields.

**Perennial weighting.** Established fruit trees, cane and bush fruit, asparagus, rhubarb and perennial herbs ride out a dry fortnight on root depth and simply do not care about a two-week outage that would gut a lettuce planting. Costs: years to establish, and the establishment years are themselves water-hungry; prime ground committed for decades; and a portfolio tilted perennial is a portfolio that cannot pivot.

**And a doctrine, inherited from the companion memo's triage order:** when the water does fail, it goes to perennials and mother stock first, seed crops second, established transplants third, field annuals last — with the addition this memo argues for: on this coast the seed crops are frequently the *same plants* as the drought-tolerant calendar, because seed wants the dry finish. A portfolio built for the worst fortnight and a portfolio built for seed production converge, and that convergence — not any single practice — is the finding.
