# The Copper IUD Without Resupply

### 380 mm² of copper is the easy part; the frame, the thread and the iron are not

*Research memo, companion in register to `research/prepper-beekeeping.md`, `research/water-systems-under-failure.md`, `research/absorbing-armed-bands.md` and `research/trapping-and-small-game.md`. Real-world facts only — not tied to any fictional setting's numbers.*

**Primed, not finished.** Sanded for grafting: sections are scoped so further research welds onto any one without disturbing the others, thin claims are flagged in place, and **Section XI names the joints left open.**

---

## I. Mechanism, and why surface area is the variable that matters

The copper IUD is not an inert foreign body with a metal decoration. The copper is the drug and the frame is the delivery vehicle.

**The mechanism is predominantly pre-fertilisation, and the literature is confident about that.** Copper ions released from the device accumulate in cervical, uterine and tubal fluid at concentrations toxic to spermatozoa, impairing motility, viability and fertilising capacity; the copper simultaneously provokes a sterile inflammatory response in the endometrium, with leukocyte and macrophage infiltration that adds a phagocytic environment hostile to gametes. The standard review is Ortiz & Croxatto, "Copper-T intrauterine device and levonorgestrel intrauterine system: biological bases of their mechanism of action," *Contraception*, 2007, vol. 75 (6 Suppl), pp. S16–S30, which states plainly that the common belief that IUDs work by destroying embryos in the uterus is not supported by the empirical evidence, and that if embryos are formed at all in the chronic presence of a copper device it happens at a far lower rate than without one.

**A post-fertilisation contribution is not excluded, and is the basis of an established secondary use.** Copper IUDs are used as emergency contraception up to five days after intercourse, which implies some capacity to act after ovulation and plausibly after fertilisation. The honest statement is: chronic use works overwhelmingly by preventing fertilisation; the emergency-contraception indication rests on a mechanism that is less cleanly characterised.

**Efficacy scales with copper surface area. That is the finding that makes copper the design variable.** Two clean demonstrations:

- Same frame, copper added or not. A ten-year randomised comparison of the inert Lippes Loop D against the identical device carrying 200 mm² of copper: cumulative accidental pregnancy 4.63 versus 0.56 per 100 women at one year, and 14.94 versus 3.62 at ten years. Expulsion/displacement also fell, 19.79 versus 6.32 (Randic, Haller, Perovic & Farr, "The effect of adding copper onto Lippes Loop IUDs: results from a ten-year study in Yugoslavia," *Contraception*, 1991, vol. 43, no. 3, pp. 229–239).
- Same frame, more copper. TCu200 against TCu380A in six countries: 12-month pregnancy 2.6 versus 0.5 per 100 women (Farr & Amatya, *Contraception*, 1994, vol. 49, no. 3, pp. 231–243).

**But blood loss does not scale with copper surface area, and this asymmetry is the whole problem.** A randomised comparison of Multiload devices carrying 250 mm² and 375 mm² found no significant difference in menstrual blood loss or in its increase at any timepoint (Milsom, Rybo & Lindstedt, *Contraception*, 1990, vol. 41, no. 3, pp. 271–281). The consequence, spelled out in Section VII: you cannot buy less bleeding by fitting less copper. You only buy more pregnancies.

## II. The device, to specification

The authoritative document is WHO/UNFPA's technical specification for the TCu380A IUD, published as Annex 10 to the *WHO Expert Committee on Specifications for Pharmaceutical Preparations, Fifty-sixth report* (WHO Technical Report Series No. 1044, 2022). It derives from ISO 7439 (*Copper-bearing contraceptive intrauterine devices — Requirements and tests*) and from Population Council NDA 18-680. Everything in the table below is quoted from that annex.

| Component | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Frame** | Low-density polyethylene, **free of stabilizers**; minimum tensile strength 13 MPa; 2% secant flexural modulus 133.5–180.6 MPa |
| **Radiopacifier** | 15–25% precipitated barium sulfate USP, particle size 95% under 10 µm |
| **Frame dimensions** | Horizontal arms 32 ± 0.5 mm total; vertical stem 36 ± 0.5 mm; arm diameter 1.6 ± 0.1 mm; stem diameter 1.5 ± 0.1 mm; terminal ball 3.0 ± 0.7 mm with a thread hole ≤ 0.80 mm |
| **Copper wire** | **Oxygen-free electronic (OFE) 99.99% pure copper, UNS C10100. No coating.** Diameter 0.255 ± 0.005 mm (30 AWG / 33 SWG); weight 176 ± 11 mg; wound on the stem; 310 mm² |
| **Copper collars** | Half-hard temper seamless tube, same OFE C10100, no coating. ID 1.68 ± 0.025 mm, OD 2.2 ± 0.025 mm, length 5 ± 0.15 mm, weight 68.7 ± 3.0 mg each; 35 mm² each; deburred and polished, e.g. by barrel tumbling; retention force ≥ 6.86 N |
| **Total copper** | Nominal 380 mm² ± 10% (tolerance per ISO 7439) |
| **Thread** | **Monofilament** high-density polyethylene, free of stabilizers, pigmented; minimum tensile strength 28 MPa; breaking force > 9.5 N; diameter 0.25 ± 0.05 mm; two tails each 105–125 mm |
| **Inserter tube** | Length 206 ± 2 mm; ID 3.7 (+0.2/−0.1) mm; OD 4.4 (+0.2/−0.1) mm |
| **Insertion rod** | Radiation-stable ABS or polypropylene; 190 ± 5 mm; tip 2.6 ± 0.2 mm |
| **Sterilisation** | Terminal, by gamma radiation (ISO 11137) or ethylene oxide (ISO 11135), within 30 days of pouch sealing; sterility assurance level 1 × 10⁻⁶ |
| **Shelf life / in situ** | Normally 5 years' shelf life (7 by agreement); **maximum in situ time 12 years** |

Three things to pull out of that.

**The "380" is verified and it is surface area, not mass.** Two collars at 35 mm² plus 310 mm² of wound wire. The geometry is internally consistent and checkable: a collar's outer lateral surface is π × 2.2 × 5 = 34.6 mm²; 176 mg of 0.255 mm copper wire is about 385 mm long and presents π × 0.255 × 385 = 308 mm². Total copper **mass** is 176 + 2 × 68.7 ≈ **313 mg** — a figure the specification never states, because mass is only a proxy for the thing that matters.

**Purity is specified at four nines and specified twice.** The annex notes that the clinical studies underpinning the device were all conducted with material complying with NDA 18-680, "which requires a minimum copper purity of 99.99%," and requires OFE C10100 for both wire and collars. It also requires that there be **no coating** on either.

**Radiopacity is convenience, not function.** The barium sulfate exists so the device can be found on X-ray. A polity with no imaging gains nothing from it — but see Section III: it is not a free omission, because the frame's mechanical specification was written for the *filled* compound, and the stiffness window of 133.5–180.6 MPa is what makes an arm that both folds into a 3.7 mm tube and springs back (memory test: arms return to within 5.0 mm of original position; flexibility test: arms deflect more than 4.0 mm under load). Pulling 15–25% mineral filler out of a polymer changes its modulus. That inference is mine, not the annex's.

## III. Can scavenged electrical wire serve?

This is where the setting's abundance meets the specification's fussiness, and the answer is a qualified no on paper and a probable yes in practice — with named contaminants that are genuinely disqualifying.

**The gap is two decimal places and three orders of magnitude of oxygen.** The spec wants UNS C10100, oxygen-free electronic: ≥ 99.99% copper, oxygen in the single parts-per-million. Ordinary electrical and building wire is UNS C11000, electrolytic tough pitch: minimum 99.90% copper plus silver, with oxygen deliberately present at roughly 200–600 ppm as cuprous oxide. ETP is the most widely used copper on earth and is what almost all scavenged conductor will be.

Nothing in the literature says ETP copper fails as an IUD. What the literature says is that the entire clinical evidence base — the 0.5 per 100 women, the twelve years, the safety profile — was generated with four-nines oxygen-free copper. The device would be off-specification in a way nobody has ever trialled. That is a different and weaker claim than "it does not work," and it should be stated as the weaker claim.

**What is actually disqualifying, in descending order of seriousness:**

- **Solder and solder residue.** Lead. Any wire that has been tinned into a joint or salvaged from a soldered assembly is out.
- **Tin plating.** Tinned copper wire is common in older and marine cable. The plating is both a physical barrier to ion release and a galvanic couple against the copper beneath. Out, unless the tin is fully removed — and verifying that without analysis is not realistic.
- **Enamel on magnet wire.** This is the cruel one. Motor and transformer windings are the most abundant source of *fine* copper wire in the right diameter range, and they are the worst candidate, because polyurethane, polyester-imide and polyamide-imide enamels are exactly the "coating" the specification forbids. Burning it off leaves carbonised residue; solvent stripping leaves film. Enamelled wire is the trap.
- **Oxygen content itself,** as a mechanical rather than toxicological problem. Copper wire in a copper IUD corrodes in three stages — rapid surface oxidation, then slower uneven corrosion, then corrosion into the core, ending in breakage — and stress corrosion cracking has been proposed as the mechanism of early rupture. Cuprous oxide inclusions are a plausible aggravator. Separately, ETP copper embrittles if annealed in a reducing atmosphere, which is a real constraint on any process that softens the wire before winding.

**The best scavenged candidate is heavy building conductor with thermoplastic insulation** — PVC or cross-linked polyethylene stripped mechanically off bare ETP copper — redrawn down to 0.255 mm. Wire drawing to that gauge is comfortably inside an 1880s floor; drawn wire of finer gauge than this was ordinary industrial practice a century earlier.

**Copper mass is not a constraint in any sense.** At 313 mg per device, one metre of 12 AWG solid conductor (about 29.6 g) is roughly ninety devices' worth; a single kilogram of scrap conductor is on the order of three thousand devices. A depopulated city's wiring is, for this purpose, effectively infinite. The arithmetic is mine; the input figures are standard wire tables.

## IV. Effectiveness, duration, and how much copper is actually consumed

**First year.** Widely quoted figures for the TCu380A are 0.6 per 100 women with perfect use and 0.8 with typical use, from Trussell's failure-rate compilations in *Contraceptive Technology*. Typical and perfect use nearly coincide because there is no user behaviour to get wrong after insertion — which is the whole point of the method and the reason it is attractive to a polity with few clinicians.

**Twelve years.** The definitive extended-use data come from two randomised multicentre trials in 24 centres, reported as "Long-term reversible contraception. Twelve years of experience with the TCu380A and TCu220C," *Contraception*, 1997, vol. 56, no. 6, pp. 341–352 (UNDP/UNFPA/WHO/World Bank Special Programme of Research, Development and Research Training in Human Reproduction). For the TCu380A: 1,396 women, 7,159 woman-years, cumulative 12-year intrauterine pregnancy **1.9 per 100 women** (SE 0.5), ectopic pregnancy 0.4 per 100 women, cumulative removals 40.2 per 100 women. **No pregnancies at all were reported after eight years of use.** The authors observe that this pregnancy rate is comparable to tubal sterilisation in the United States.

That result is the single most important fact in this memo for a resource-poor polity: the licensed ten years is conservative, the WHO specification now permits twelve, and the failure curve flattens rather than climbing. A device fitted at 28 does not need replacing at 38.

**Does the copper get used up? Yes, but not within its service life.** The mechanism is dissolution, so copper is consumed. Measured on 118 explanted TCu220C devices in utero for 12 to 72 months, mean daily copper loss was **29.4 µg/day**, range 7.3 to 62.1, with an inverse correlation between the extent of mineral deposit on the copper surface and the dissolution rate (Thiery, Schmidt & Tatum, "Copper loss from the Copper T model TCu220C," *Contraception*, 1982, vol. 26). Release is not flat: there is an initial burst on the order of a few hundred µg/day falling to roughly 40 µg/day within one to two months.

Taking the mean at face value gives about 107 mg over a decade. Scaling by surface area from the TCu220C's 220 mm² to the TCu380A's 380 mm² gives roughly 51 µg/day and about 185 mg over a decade — against 313 mg present. Somewhere between a third and three-fifths of the copper is gone at ten years. **That scaling is mine, not the paper's**, and the two device geometries differ (the TCu220C carries sleeves, not wound wire).

**The failure mode of long use is mechanical, not exhaustion.** Copper wire on IUDs breaks, and the incidence rises with duration: earliest breakages at 8 months for 0.30 mm wire and 9 months for 0.40 mm, with a risk on the order of 30% during the fourth year, reported in "Duration of use of copper releasing IUDs and the incidence of copper wire breakage," *European Journal of Obstetrics, Gynecology and Reproductive Biology*, 1990. Some authors on that basis recommended replacing wire-wound TCu-200 devices every three years. The TCu380A's design is partly an answer to this: the collars are solid tube, and they persist. A design that carries all its copper as wound thin wire trades manufacturing simplicity for a shorter honest service life. Solid sleeves are the more durable geometry and are also harder to make.

## V. Insertion: instruments, asepsis, and who may hold the tenaculum

**The instrument list is short and almost entirely 1880s work.**

| Instrument | Improvisable at an 1880s machining floor? |
|---|---|
| Bivalve speculum | Yes — Sims' speculum dates to 1845, the bivalve to the same era |
| Single-tooth tenaculum / vulsellum | Yes — trivial forging and grinding |
| Uterine sound (graduated, curved) | Yes — a graduated malleable probe; Simpson's sound is 19th century |
| Long dressing/sponge forceps, long curved scissors | Yes |
| Antiseptic, gloves, light source | Yes; gloves are the awkward one |
| **Inserter tube + rod + flange** | **This is the hard part** |

The inserter is a thin-walled tube of internal diameter 3.7 mm and outside diameter 4.4 mm — a wall of about 0.35 mm — 206 mm long, which must be smooth-ended, hold a folded T frame, release it on withdrawal, and not trap the thread. Drawn thin-wall metal tube of that dimension is within reach; a *sterile single-use plastic* inserter is not. A reusable metal inserter is a different device with a different injury profile against the cervix and fundus, and no trial data.

**Sterilisation: what is actually necessary is less than an autoclave, and the standards say so explicitly.** The relevant distinction is sterilisation versus high-level disinfection.

- **Steam autoclave.** Recognised minimum exposure for wrapped supplies is 30 minutes at 121 °C in a gravity-displacement sterilizer, or 4 minutes at 132 °C in a prevacuum sterilizer (CDC, *Guideline for Disinfection and Sterilization in Healthcare Facilities*, 2008). **A domestic pressure cooker at 15 psi gauge reaches 121 °C** and is a genuine gravity-displacement steam sterilizer in miniature — this is the single most useful substitution in the whole memo.
- **Dry heat.** 170 °C for 60 minutes, 160 °C for 120 minutes, 150 °C for 150 minutes. Suitable for sharp instruments and anything moist heat would damage. An oven with a reliable thermometer does this.
- **Boiling.** 100 °C for 20 minutes is **high-level disinfection, not sterilisation.** Boiling water is inadequate against bacterial spores; CDC characterises it as closer to pasteurisation.

The decisive point is that IUD insertion does not require sterilisation. WHO's *Family Planning: A Global Handbook for Providers* specifies "high-level disinfected or sterile instruments," lists boiling and chemical soaking among acceptable high-level disinfection methods, and states that routine antibiotic prophylaxis is generally not recommended for women at low risk of STI. What does the real work is **no-touch technique**: load the device inside its unopened package, clean the cervix and adjacent vagina with antiseptic, pass the sound and then the loaded inserter through the cervical canal **once each**, and avoid contact with the vaginal walls or speculum in between.

**Who may insert — the binding constraint is softer than it looks.** WHO's 2012 *Recommendations: Optimizing Health Worker Roles to Improve Access to Key Maternal and Newborn Health Interventions Through Task Shifting* (OptimizeMNH) addresses IUD insertion and removal by nurses, midwives and auxiliary nurse-midwives. The published evidence-base framework for the nurses question is blunt about what it costs: the resource requirements are listed as **"minimal training for nurses to insert and remove an IUD," regular supervision by a senior midwife or doctor, and referral for a small number of women.** The certainty ratings are modest — low for expulsion and continuation, very low for removal rates, unintended pregnancy and complications, moderate for less pain at insertion with nurses.

The underlying trial evidence is Polus, Lewin, Glenton, Lerberg, Rehfuess & Gülmezoglu, "Optimizing the delivery of contraceptives in low- and middle-income countries through task shifting: a systematic review of effectiveness and safety," *Reproductive Health*, 2015, vol. 12, art. 27. Nurses versus doctors: continuation 99% versus 79%, expulsion 5% versus 5.4%, unintended pregnancy 0.8% versus 1.2%, pain at insertion 7% versus 10.8%. Auxiliary nurse-midwives versus doctors: continuation 72.7% versus 69.9%, expulsion 8.1% versus 9.6%, pregnancy 1.9% versus 2.0%.

**One outcome breaks the pattern, and it is the one that matters for a small polity: insertion failure in nulliparous women was 11.7% for nurses against 3.4% for doctors, relative risk 3.41.** The physician advantage is concentrated in the difficult cervix, not in routine work.

A more recent field cohort agrees on the shape: Mhlanga, Balkus, Singh et al., "Feasibility and Safety of IUD Insertion by Mid-Level Providers in Sub-Saharan Africa," *International Perspectives on Sexual and Reproductive Health*, 2019, vol. 45, pp. 61–69, reported expulsion of 12.3 per 100 woman-years for trained study nurses, 11.9 for local non-study providers, and 7.3 for study physicians, across 535 women in Malawi, South Africa, Uganda and Zimbabwe.

**Synthesis, mine rather than any source's:** task-sharing dissolves the physician constraint for routine insertion in parous women, and leaves it standing for three things — the difficult nulliparous insertion, the removal with no visible thread, and the management of a perforation. Physician scarcity therefore does not cap the *programme*; it caps the *exceptions*, and the exceptions are where the harm is.

## VI. Risks, with numbers

**Pelvic inflammatory disease: the window is real and it is about three weeks.** The reference is Farley, Rosenberg, Rowe, Chen & Meirik, "Intrauterine devices and pelvic inflammatory disease: an international perspective," *The Lancet*, 1992, vol. 339, no. 8796, pp. 785–788 — a WHO analysis of 22,908 IUD insertions across 51,399 woman-years of follow-up, with an overall PID rate of 1.6 cases per 1,000 woman-years. PID incidence was **9.7 per 1,000 woman-years during the 20 days after insertion versus 1.4 per 1,000 woman-years thereafter**, a more than six-fold difference after adjustment, and the later rate was low and constant for up to eight years of follow-up. Rates varied by geography (highest in Africa, lowest in China) and inversely with age.

Read that carefully, because it inverts the folk belief: the device is not an infection risk. The *insertion* is, briefly, and in proportion to the background prevalence of sexually transmitted infection in the population. In a closed polity with low STI prevalence and clean no-touch technique, the residual PID risk of a copper IUD is on the order of one or two cases per thousand woman-years — which is to say, negligible against the maternal mortality it displaces.

**Uterine perforation: roughly one per thousand insertions.** The European Active Surveillance Study on Intrauterine Devices found 1.1 perforations per 1,000 insertions for copper IUDs (95% CI 0.7–1.7), 1.4 for levonorgestrel systems, 1.3 overall (Heinemann, Reed, Moehner & Do Minh, *Contraception*, 2015, vol. 91, no. 4, pp. 274–279). **Breastfeeding at insertion carried a sixfold increase in perforation risk across both device types** (RR 6.1, 95% CI 3.9–9.6), which in a polity with long lactational intervals is not a footnote.

**Expulsion is the commonest mechanical failure and it is front-loaded.** Comparative trials put first-year expulsion of the TCu380A around 5%, with about 8.9% over 12 months in nulliparous women. Immediate postpartum insertion is far worse — one Brazilian university-hospital series reported 25.6% at six months. The Yugoslav data give the copper-versus-inert contrast on an identical frame: expulsion/displacement 4.5% at one year with copper against 12.6% without, and 6.3% versus 19.8% at ten years. Expulsion clusters in the first months and is associated with mismatch between device and cavity size.

**Ectopic pregnancy: low absolute risk, high conditional risk.** The WHO 12-year trial recorded 0.4 ectopic pregnancies per 100 women over twelve years with the TCu380A. But when a pregnancy does occur with a device in situ, secondary sources put the proportion that are ectopic at 15–50%. Both facts are true and they point opposite ways: the device makes ectopic pregnancy rarer in absolute terms and makes any pregnancy-with-device far more likely to be ectopic. Without imaging or serial hCG, that conditional risk cannot be worked up.

**Malposition and wire breakage** are covered in Sections IV and IX. Malposition short of expulsion is, in a no-imaging setting, largely undiagnosable.

## VII. The bleeding problem, and the iron ledger

This is the section that matters most, and the reason is not the bleeding itself. It is that the reassuring literature on iron rests on a physiological adaptation that requires dietary iron the population does not have.

**The magnitude is well measured and consistent across devices and decades.** Menstrual blood loss is quantified by alkaline haematin extraction, not by self-report, in the studies below.

| Study | Device | MBL before | MBL after | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Milsom, Andersson, Jonasson, Lindstedt & Rybo, *Contraception*, 1995, 52(3):175–179 (n=18) | Gyne-T 380S | 59 ± 8 mL | 91 ± 11 mL at 3 mo; 94 ± 12 at 6; 92 ± 13 at 12 | 54–59% |
| Milsom, Rybo & Lindstedt, *Contraception*, 1990, 41(3):271–281 | MLCu-250 | 54.4 ± 10.3 mL | 86.4 ± 10.3 mL at 3 mo | ~59% |
| same | MLCu-375 | 56.9 ± 6.9 mL | 81.1 ± 8.3 mL at 3 mo | ~43% |
| Larsson, Milsom, Jonasson, Lindstedt & Rybo, *Contraception*, 1993, 48(5):471–480 (n=25, 3 yr) | MLCu-250 / MLCu-375 | ~55–59 mL | sustained | 55/49% at 3 mo; 64/41% at 12 mo; 47/39% at 36 mo |

**The increase appears within three months and does not resolve.** It is roughly 40–60% of baseline, or about 25–35 mL per cycle, and it is still there at three years.

**Reframed, that is the finding.** The classical clinical threshold for menorrhagia is 80 mL per cycle. A copper IUD moves a woman with an ordinary 55–60 mL period to 85–95 mL. **On average, the device converts a normal menstrual loss into a clinically heavy one, by definition.** That framing is mine; the numbers are the sources'.

**The iron arithmetic — mine, from standard inputs.** Whole blood carries about 0.5 mg of iron per mL. An additional 30 mL per cycle is an additional **15 mg of iron per cycle**, or roughly **195 mg per year** over thirteen cycles. Total menstrual iron loss rises from something like 28–30 mg per cycle to about 45. To stay in balance, intestinal absorption must increase by roughly 0.5 mg per day, sustained, for as long as the device is in place. On a diet dominated by non-haem iron with phytate present and no reliable ascorbate outside the growing season, fractional absorption of 5% or less is plausible, which makes the required *dietary* increment on the order of 10 mg per day. That is not a rounding error on a calorie-constrained diet.

**And the reassuring studies are all Swedish, and they say why.** Larsson 1993 concluded that women "from developed countries apparently tolerate an increased MBL of approximately 45% without developing anemia," and that iron stores were unchanged "indicating an adequate adaptive increase in intestinal iron absorption." Milsom 1995 concluded that findings may differ where dietary iron availability is lower. **The mechanism of the reassurance is the adaptation, and the adaptation is the thing that fails first in an iron-poor population.** This is the pivot of the whole memo and it is stated by the primary authors themselves, not imposed on them.

**What the broader evidence says at the population level.** A systematic review of haemoglobin and ferritin in IUD users (*Contraception*, 2013, vol. 87, no. 4, pp. 486–496) found statistically significant mean decreases in haemoglobin among copper-IUD users, with ferritin following the same pattern, but decreases not sufficient to induce anaemia in previously non-anaemic women. In women who are *already* anaemic, Tepper, Steenland, Marchbanks & Curtis, "Hemoglobin measurement prior to initiating copper intrauterine devices: a systematic review," *Contraception*, 2013, vol. 87, no. 5, pp. 639–644, found one randomised trial and one prospective cohort showing no significant haemoglobin change, and two cohorts showing a statistically significant but clinically small mean decrease over twelve months. Both US and WHO medical eligibility criteria classify known anaemia as **category 2** for the copper IUD — advantages generally outweigh risks — with clinical follow-up advised.

Note what "clinical follow-up advised" presupposes: haemoglobin measurement. A polity that cannot measure haemoglobin cannot execute the mitigation the guidelines specify, and will not detect the deficit until it presents as fatigue, breathlessness and poor obstetric outcomes years later.

**Is bleeding the main driver of discontinuation? Yes, with pain.** Bleeding and pain are consistently the leading medical reason for removal. In the first year of use, reported unwanted effects run to roughly 19% bleeding and 17% pain. A randomised comparison against the levonorgestrel system found 9.7 per 100 women discontinuing the copper device for heavy bleeding and dysmenorrhoea against 1.3 per 100 for the hormonal device (this pairing reached me through a secondary summary and is not traced to its primary trial). The WHO 12-year trial's cumulative 40.2 removals per 100 women is dominated by this category.

**The only real mitigation is an NSAID, and that is a pharmacopoeia question.** Godfrey et al., "Treatment of bleeding irregularities in women with copper-containing IUDs: a systematic review," *Contraception*, 2013, found that eight of ten studies reported statistically significant **20% to 45% reductions in mean menstrual blood loss** with NSAIDs versus no treatment or placebo, including trials of mefenamic acid; antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid were comparable to mefenamic acid over 90 days but with poorly documented safety in small samples.

A 20–45% reduction in blood loss would largely cancel the device's penalty. The problem is the molecule. Mefenamic acid, naproxen and ibuprofen are mid-twentieth-century syntheses. Tranexamic acid is 1960s. **Acetylsalicylic acid is reachable at an 1880s–1890s chemical floor** — Kolbe's salicylic acid synthesis is 1874, Bayer's aspirin 1897 — but aspirin is antiplatelet as well as anti-inflammatory, the evidence for it in menorrhagia is weak and mixed, and it is the one NSAID that might plausibly make bleeding worse. **This is a thin claim flagged in place: I found no trial of aspirin specifically for copper-IUD menorrhagia, and the pharmacological argument cuts both ways.** It is the single most consequential gap in this memo.

## VIII. Historical precedent: devices made without modern manufacturing

The record here is unusually informative, because the pre-plastic IUD was tried at scale and the results were written down.

**Gräfenberg's ring (1929) was made of the materials an 1880s floor has.** Ernst Gräfenberg's first devices were coils of **silkworm gut**; he then wound silkworm gut with **silver wire**; he then moved to a ring of **"German silver" — an alloy of copper, nickel and zinc** — reportedly because pure silver discoloured the gums. It had **no tail**. The standard historical account is Thiery, "Intrauterine contraception: from silver ring to intrauterine contraceptive implant," *European Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology and Reproductive Biology*, 2000, vol. 90, no. 2, pp. 145–152.

**The retrospective irony is load-bearing for this memo: German silver contains copper.** The first IUD used by a significant number of women was, unwittingly, a copper-bearing IUD, and the copper content is a plausible part of why it worked at all. That is a reconstruction rather than a demonstrated fact, and should be flagged as such.

**Its clinical record was poor, and we know how poor.** Caroline Rusterholz, "Testing the Gräfenberg Ring in Interwar Britain: Norman Haire, Helena Wright, and the Debate over Statistical Evidence, Side Effects, and Intra-uterine Contraception," *Journal of the History of Medicine and Allied Sciences*, 2017, vol. 72, no. 4, pp. 448–467, documents Helena Wright's series for the Birth Control Investigation Committee: **only 9 of her 38 patients still retained the ring at publication, and one required hysterectomy after infection.** Wright described the method as offering uncertain protection and warned about a foreign body liable to undergo both chemical change and physical fragmentation. By 1933 major medical conferences had concluded the risks were unacceptable, and the ring left wide use around 1939.

Two conclusions follow, and they point in opposite directions. The ring's **expulsion** problem is structural — a closed metal ring has no shape memory to fold into an inserter and no arms to hold the fundus — and would recur in any polity that reinvented it. The ring's **infection** problem was a pre-antibiotic problem. Under penicillin-level medicine with Farley's 20-day window understood, the historical objection that killed the method largely dissolves.

**The Ota ring (Japan, 1930s)** was Tenrei Ota's development of the same idea, adding a central supporting structure, made in gold and gold-plated versions. It saw large-scale use in Japan.

**The Lippes Loop (1962)** was the first-generation inert plastic device — polyethylene with barium sulfate, monofilament nylon tail, four sequential curves for retention. Its performance is the honest baseline for what a frame can do with **no copper at all**: cumulative pregnancy 4.63 per 100 women at one year and 14.94 at ten years, against 3.62 at ten years for the same loop carrying 200 mm² of copper (Randic et al. 1991). A polity that can make a frame but not usable copper is looking at roughly one pregnancy per seven women per decade.

**The Dalkon Shield (1971) failed for a reason an improvising polity would reproduce exactly.** Its **multifilament** tail string, sheathed in nylon-6 and chosen for tensile strength, wicked vaginal fluid and bacteria up into the uterus, bypassing the antibacterial action of cervical mucus. The nylon sheath hydrolysed in situ, opening further entry points. When a user conceived, the expanding uterus drew the colonised tail upward, producing septic mid-trimester abortion; women died. The device was withdrawn in 1974 and the litigation ultimately bankrupted A.H. Robins.

**The lesson is a manufacturing lesson, and it is the one most likely to be got wrong.** A braided or twisted thread is stronger, easier to make from natural fibre, and easier to knot than a monofilament. It is also the specific feature that killed people. The WHO specification's demand for a **monofilament** HDPE thread of 0.25 mm with breaking force above 9.5 N is not fussiness; it is a headstone.

## IX. Removal, and the device you cannot find

**Routine removal is trivial and needs less than insertion.** Speculum, narrow forceps, traction on the threads; no dilation, no anaesthesia, no sound. It is the easiest part of the method and is within the same task-shared scope as insertion.

**Missing threads are common.** Roughly 4–18% of users will at some point have non-visible strings, most often simple retraction of the thread into the cervical canal. Standard management is a sequence: sweep the endocervical canal with a cytology brush, which recovers the threads in most cases; then narrow alligator or Kelly forceps, or a purpose-made retriever. Published series using ultrasound guidance report successful office removal in over 97% of no-string cases, including embedded devices.

**The standard of care contains the exact instruction a no-imaging polity cannot follow: confirm the device is in the uterus before instrumenting.** Without imaging, four situations present identically as a woman with no visible threads:

1. The device was expelled and she did not notice.
2. The threads have retracted into the canal or cavity; device correctly sited.
3. The device is embedded in the myometrium.
4. The device perforated and lies in the peritoneal cavity.

There is no bedside test that separates these. A blind instrumentation of case 1 traumatises an empty uterus for nothing; of case 3 or 4 it makes matters worse. Case 4 classically requires laparoscopy or laparotomy for retrieval, and at roughly one perforation per thousand insertions it will occur at any programme scale that matters. Copper wire fragmentation adds a fifth possibility: retained copper fragments after an otherwise successful removal, which are reported in the literature and are again a radiographic diagnosis.

**And the same blindness compounds the ectopic problem.** A woman with amenorrhoea, no visible threads and abdominal pain cannot be worked up. She is simultaneously a possible intrauterine pregnancy with a device in situ, a possible ectopic, and a possible expulsion followed by ordinary pregnancy, and the management of those three diverges sharply.

## X. Is there a hard cap?

Following the companion memos: is there a limit no amount of skill exceeds?

**Not in the copper.** Three hundred and thirteen milligrams per device, from a city's worth of conductor. Purity is off-specification and the honest position is that ETP copper is untrialled rather than known-bad; the disqualifying contaminants — solder, tin plate, enamel — are identifiable by eye and by which cable you cut. This is the input everyone assumes is the bottleneck and it is not close to being one.

**Not in the machining.** Every instrument in the insertion tray is nineteenth-century surgical work. The pressure cooker is a gravity-displacement autoclave and the accepted standard for IUD insertion is high-level disinfection anyway, which open boiling achieves. Fine-wire drawing to 0.255 mm is a solved problem.

**Not in the operator.** The WHO task-sharing evidence base states the training requirement for a nurse to insert and remove an IUD as *minimal*, with supervision and a referral pathway, and the trial outcomes for nurses match physicians on expulsion, continuation and pregnancy. Physician scarcity caps the exceptions — the nulliparous insertion, the missing thread, the perforation — not the programme.

**The cap is the frame and the thread.** Low-density polyethylene is a 1933 material and high-density polyethylene a 1953 one; a monofilament synthetic thread of 0.25 mm with a 9.5 N breaking force is likewise mid-century. An 1880s floor has celluloid, ebonite, catgut, silk and silkworm gut, and metal. Every substitution has a known failure mode: a metal ring cannot fold into an inserter and expels (9 of 38 retained, in Wright's series); absorbable gut wicks and disappears; natural braided fibre is the Dalkon Shield's mechanism of death. Silkworm gut is a genuine natural monofilament and Gräfenberg used it — which makes it the most interesting single graft point in this memo — but it is an animal product with limited shelf life, and no shape-memory frame comes with it.

**And the programme cap is iron.** Copper's efficacy scales with surface area; copper's blood loss does not. The polity therefore cannot dial the bleeding down without dialling the protection down. It faces a device that on average converts a normal period into a clinically heavy one, in a population that is already iron-poor, with no ferrous sulfate, no reliable NSAID, and no haemoglobin assay to detect the drift. The literature that says copper IUDs do not cause anaemia is Scandinavian and says explicitly that the reason is an adaptive rise in intestinal iron absorption — which is precisely the compensation an iron-poor, calorie-constrained diet cannot fund.

If a single sentence is wanted: **the copper is free and the surgery is nineteenth-century, so the device fails on the frame and the thread, which are mid-twentieth-century polymers, and the programme fails on iron, because the mechanism that makes the reassuring bleeding literature reassuring is dietary iron the population does not have.**

A second near-cap, on the pattern of the other memos: **with no imaging there is no diagnosis of the missing thread.** Expelled, retracted, embedded and translocated into the peritoneum are one clinical presentation, so the follow-up half of the method carries an irreducible blind spot no operator skill closes — and at roughly one perforation per thousand insertions, every programme of any size eventually generates a question it cannot ask.

## XI. Joints left open for grafting

1. **The levonorgestrel IUS as the counterfactual.** It *reduces* menstrual blood loss and raises haemoglobin, inverting Section VII entirely. What steroid-synthesis floor it requires is the question that decides whether Section VII is a permanent constraint or a temporary one. Not covered at all.
2. **The comparator methods.** Barrier methods and their manufacture, lactational amenorrhoea, fertility awareness, withdrawal, and above all **vasectomy** — which plausibly has a far lower manufacturing floor than any IUD and moves the burden off the iron-deficient party. Conspicuously absent here.
3. **Iron nutrition from first principles.** Ferrous sulfate manufacture from scrap iron and sulfuric acid, dietary iron on the British Columbia coast (shellfish, salmon, seaweed, nettle), phytate and ascorbate interactions, and what a realistic absorbable-iron budget looks like. Section VII's arithmetic is only as good as this.
4. **The analgesic and NSAID floor.** Whether anything better than aspirin is reachable, whether aspirin helps or harms here, and what dysmenorrhoea management looks like with opium and willow bark. Flagged in place as the memo's biggest single gap.
5. **The polymer floor.** Could a surviving university make polyethylene at all? High-pressure LDPE (ICI, 1933) versus Ziegler–Natta HDPE (1953), and the pre-polyolefin candidates — celluloid, cellulose acetate, casein plastics, vulcanised rubber — assessed as frame materials rather than mentioned as options.
6. **Silkworm gut and natural monofilament.** Sericulture, gut preparation, shelf life, sterilisability, and whether a tail can be made that is monofilament without being synthetic. The Dalkon lesson makes this the load-bearing question of Section VIII.
7. **Instrument metallurgy.** Stainless steel versus nickel-plated carbon steel, resharpening, re-plating, and corrosion in repeated steam cycles.
8. **Pain, anaesthesia and consent.** Paracervical block with procaine (1905) or cocaine (earlier), what unanaesthetised insertion does to uptake, and how consent works in a polity that has an interest in the outcome. Entirely absent, and probably the most important omission for anything other than a technical brief.
9. **STI epidemiology in a closed population.** Farley makes background STI prevalence, not the device, the determinant of PID risk. A small closed polity's prevalence — and what happens when armed bands are absorbed into it — is a completely different question and directly connects to `research/absorbing-armed-bands.md`.
10. **Insertion timing.** Postpartum and post-abortion insertion carry large expulsion penalties but catch women who are already present. The trade-off is quantifiable and is not quantified here.
11. **Quality assurance without a standards regime.** How a lab verifies 380 mm², wire purity, sterility assurance and cytotoxicity with no calibration chain, no bioburden testing and no accredited laboratory. The specification in Section II presumes an entire institutional apparatus.
12. **Records and the twelve-year clock.** Who holds the insertion register, what happens at twelve years, what happens if the register is lost, and whether a woman can be relied on to know her own insertion date decades later.
13. **Copper toxicity and total copper burden.** Systemic absorption from the device is contested but small; the interesting question is cumulative exposure in a polity that has also reverted to copper vessels and copper plumbing, plus Wilson disease as an unscreened contraindication.
