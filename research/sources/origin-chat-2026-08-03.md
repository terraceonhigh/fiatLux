Reconciled nobledark register with institutional machinery through naming and sacrifice

Then the register isn't a softening, and it's worth seeing why it fits so exactly. The long horizon is the *native* academic timescale — a supervisor's entire function is to produce someone who will surpass them after they are dead, and the institution's ordinary business is planting things it won't sit under. Rothamsted has run the same grass plots since 1856. Beal buried seed bottles to be opened by people who hadn't been born. Vavilov's staff starved surrounded by edible seed in Leningrad because the collection was for the next century and they were only in this one. "To die, not seeing the fruit" is not a heroic exception to institutional life; it is the ordinary condition of it, and you've simply found the register in which that reads as glory rather than as a pension problem.

Which means your martyrology already has its machinery, and it's the machinery you flagged at the start. Naming. Endowed chairs, named lectures, plaques, buildings — a university is a calendar of saints that has forgotten it is one. I told you earlier that every institution in the setting would be named for a donor whose money came from something nobody discusses. Invert it and the mechanism is untouched: the Vavilov Greenhouse, the Greenwich Lecture delivered on a fixed night whether or not anyone comes, the Locke Assize. Same statutes, same committee, opposite content. And the names you've chosen are themselves a device — a polity that names its founders after people the old world took from and did not credit, with Lacks riding circuit because her mother died for want of one. The hagiography as corrected ledger. Say it once in the text and never explain it.

The reframe I'd take furthest is §VI. In this register the accreditation empire isn't vassalage, it's the **respublica literaria** — the seventeenth-century republic of letters, correspondence between distant institutions carried by whoever is sailing that way, three months each direction, every letter possibly arriving after its author's death. Greenwich keeping the twenty-metre band open because ionospheric skip doesn't require anything in between and never stopped working. Plutarch's chronometer is the right object precisely because a chronometer is longitude, and longitude is the ability to *find* people again. Same structure as before — legitimacy travelling inside people, sovereignty as gradient — with the valence entirely reversed. Colleagueship rather than charter.

One craft note, offered as candor rather than as a plea for the grim. Nobledark's failure mode isn't cosiness, it's hagiography without cost: martyrs remembered, but the institution never has to *choose*. Keep the minutes and keep the vote counts — but let the votes be between two goods. The circuit that Locke died on was funded at the expense of something, and the people who voted against it were not cowards, and the greenhouse was held that winter at a price paid by people who are also named. Tragic choice, not villainy. That's tofu-firm, and it's Macaulay's own setting: Horatius holds the bridge because the bridge must come down behind him, which is an engineering decision taken by a committee under time pressure.

And note what Macaulay was actually doing — inventing lost ballads on the theory that Rome must once have sung its history before the annalists dried it out. Your Academy will produce exactly that, about two centuries in: a Literature faculty reconstructing the founding in verse because the archive is minutes, and minutes don't sing. Bad history, exact emotionally, taught to every child in the province. The historians will publish a devastating and completely correct rebuttal, and lose, and the poem will be what the polity means by itself.

Four choices in here you may want to reverse:

**The anniversary is the vote, not the death.** Everything else follows from that — the lecture is an argument about minutes rather than a eulogy, which is the only way I could find to get tragic choice into a founding myth without villainy.
