Locke and Plutarch and Greenwich don't fit that pattern cleanly, so either the rule is looser than I've stated it or those names arrive by a different logic.

## What this revises in the three briefs

### 1. The chronology in `four-beats.md` is void — and the reason is worse than the error

You told me to disregard it, and you're right, but the interesting part is *why* it fails. I built a hundred-year arc. Canon runs to **year 1080 across ten phases**, and the story sits somewhere well short of that. So the scale isn't centennial, it's millennial, and my "nobody from the apocalypse is alive anymore" is not a landmark at all — it's a condition that obtains for nine hundred years.

I've marked the table dead in place rather than deleting it; the *relative* sequencing still holds and the absolute years don't.

**The much larger problem it exposes: the 1880s floor caps my technology, and my briefs describe a peak.**

Phase 7 descends to an 1880s technological floor. Line the briefs up against that:

| Capability | Real-world era | Survives the 1880s floor? |
|---|---|---|
| Chronometers, sextants, dividing engines, almanacs | 1760s–1850s | **Yes.** Comfortably below the floor |
| Sailing schooners, ropewalks, copper sheathing | 1800s–1880s | **Yes** |
| Antisepsis, ether, aspirin, smallpox vaccine, horse antitoxin | 1840s–1890s | **Yes**, mostly — antitoxin is right at the line |
| Sulfonamides | 1930s | **No** |
| **Penicillin, deep-tank fermentation** | **1940s** | **No** |
| **Radio** | **1900s onward** | **No** |

So my penicillin brief and the radio half of beat 4 describe capability that sits *above* the floor the polity later falls through. That reframes both briefs, and reframes them in a way that's better than what I wrote:

- **The penicillin brief is not a description of how the polity works. It's a description of what the polity loses in Phase 7.** Every chokepoint I ranked by tightness is now a *timeline of the descent* — the strain degenerates, the solvent still fails, the blowers stop, and the polity walks back down the list in reverse order until it's at sulfa, then at antisepsis and surgery. "Bereavement rather than decadence" is exactly right, and the ranked chokepoint list is the order in which the bereavement arrives.
- **Beat 3's instruments survive the floor and beat 4's radio does not.** Chronometers, sextants and the almanac are all comfortably pre-1880. Radio is not. So Greenwich keeping the twenty-metre band open is either before the descent, or a recovered capability, or the single anachronistic thing the polity holds onto — and if it's the third, that's a wonderful asymmetry: **a polity that can hear the world but has lost antibiotics.**

This is the biggest open question and I've put it at the top of the list below.

### 2. Beat 1 — the vote outranks the death, and the door is thermal

Two canon facts break my framing and replace it with a better one.

**"The anniversary is the vote, not the death"** subordinates everything I wrote about martyrdom to a procedural question. My material on the multiplier arithmetic — a seed potato eaten is one meal, planted it returns five to fifteen times its weight — stops being the martyr's private justification and becomes **the case put to a deliberative body that then divided nineteen to twenty-three.** That's much stronger. The arithmetic is a floor speech.

And Odede is the mechanism that makes it nobledark rather than hagiography: **he ran the ration kitchen, voted no, and forged his own ration downward.** He thought holding the greenhouse was wrong and starved himself anyway. That's the whole register in one man, and it means the vote's losing side is *not* the cowardly side. My brief's instruction to "voice the counter-argument through someone sympathetic" is already canon and already better executed than I proposed it.

**"Thermal, not martial"** is the correction that redeems my most technical section. I wrote the seed bank as a working building with a drying fire, desiccant, and a door people come to — and I reached for a barricade to explain the death at the doors. Canon deletes the mob and makes the door a **heat leak**. That's better, and my germplasm research supports it precisely:

- The collection needs cold and it needs dry. Every opening of the door exchanges conditioned air for wet Cascadian air and costs both.
- So the door has a **budget**, measured in openings, and someone keeps the tally.
- **The flue** is presumably how heat is managed — a fire for regenerating desiccant, or a stove whose heat must be kept *out* of the seed room while keeping the building above freezing. Either way the flue is the thermal control and the key is the access control, and those are the two objects canon names.
- Vavilov holds a key to a room full of edible seed in a famine, and the heroism is that **he opens it as rarely as possible.** Not defiance — accountancy. Small technical heroism, exactly as the register specifies.
- **Accession 4,118** is then almost certainly a specific accession that had to be grown out or lost, which is what forces the door open at a cost. My grow-out material is the mechanism: an accession with a clock on it, in a famine winter, requiring a planting.

**One invention of mine that needs re-pointing, not scrapping: the posthumous conferral.** I proposed the martyr dies uncredentialed and the state's first sovereign act is conferring a degree on a corpse — which is why convocation becomes the citizenship rite. Canon puts a vote at the centre instead of a death, so the conferral can't be the founding act. Three ways to keep it:

- Attach it to **the twenty-three.** The unnamed losing side receives something posthumously, and the wall's inversion is that gesture. This fits "inversion of the wall" better than anything else I can construct.
- Attach it to **Odede** specifically, who is owed something the polity cannot name.
- Retire it, and let the citizenship rite descend from the Division instead — you are a citizen because you are entered in a register, and the founding register is a division list.

The third is probably the cleanest and it costs me my favourite idea, which is usually a sign it's correct.

### 3. Beat 2 — the circuit is already canon, and it has a name

**Lacks rides circuit "because her mother died for want of one."** So the circuit exists in canon and its founding motive is a specific death, and my entire beat-2 brief is background for a thing already established. Nothing in it contradicts canon as far as I can see. The pieces that now attach to something:

- **The Locke Assize** is the judicial circuit; Lacks's is the medical one. Same route, probably the same calendar, possibly the same escort. Two arms of the capital travelling the same road is an efficiency any bursar would insist on and a jurisdictional mess any lawyer would hate.
- **The clinical debt argued in Senate** is my "circuit does five jobs at once" material with the accounting made explicit and public. Good — the debt is the price of the fifth job.
- **The 1:40 ratio** I can't interpret without more. If it's physician-to-population it's implausibly good; if it's rider-to-village or graduate-to-cohort it's a workload. If it's what I suspect — one trained practitioner per forty settlements, or one circuit year per forty patients who needed one — then it's the number that makes the whole thing grim, and the export's flag makes sense.
- My **counterfeit-rider** material now has a home: verifiable credentials in an illiterate province, and the *respublica literaria* runs on exactly that problem at longer range.

### 4. Beats 3 and 4 — *respublica literaria* is a better frame than mine, and it's not charity

I framed the voyage as a polity discovering it has a foreign policy. Canon's frame is stronger: **the far city is a colleague, not a stranger.** Correspondence at three months each direction, letters arriving after their authors have died, distant institutions in a republic of letters that never formally dissolved. The ship is not a relief mission to an unknown. It is a **visit**, overdue by a generation, to people whose predecessors your predecessors corresponded with.

That changes the emotional register of the beat completely and for the better. It also means:

- **My verification-problem material is the load-bearing thing in the brief**, not a garnish. A republic of letters conducted at three months' latency, between institutions that cannot confirm each other's existence, runs entirely on authentication — potency, weights, time, credentials, a callsign, a seal, a recognizable fist on a key. That *is* the *respublica literaria*, mechanically. "Authentication is what this polity exports" turns out to be the setting's thesis rather than my observation.
- **"Longitude is the ability to find people again"** is the best line in the export and it's the answer to why Engineering makes chronometers at all. Not trade, not exploration. Finding people. My four-seconds-per-nautical-mile arithmetic is in service of that, and Plutarch's chronometer should probably have a rate certificate with a chain of signatures going back decades — every rating a small act of institutional continuity.
- **My "receive is cheap, transmit is expensive" asymmetry needs qualifying.** Canon has Greenwich keeping the twenty-metre band open, which means a real transmitter at the standards institution. So the asymmetry holds for everyone *except* Greenwich, which makes Greenwich's transmitter a strategic asset and its silence a diplomatic event. My solar-cycle point gets sharper here: Greenwich can keep the band open and the band can still close, and no one is at fault, and the relationship goes quiet for years.

## Two anchors from the export that beat anything in my briefs

The export names two real long-horizon experiments I should have had and didn't. Both are better than my examples because both are still running:

- **Rothamsted, Park Grass, since 1856.** The same plots, the same treatments, continuously, for over a century and a half. An institution's ordinary business is a data series longer than any career in it.
- **The Beal seed viability experiment, Michigan State, 1879.** W.J. Beal buried bottles of seed to be dug up on a schedule by people who had not been born. It is still being dug. **This is a seed bank designed from the outset to be operated by strangers**, which is exactly the polity's problem, solved in advance, by someone who understood he would not see the result.

Beal in particular belongs in the seed-bank material. It's the real-world proof that the long-horizon custodianship the register requires is not a fantasy of virtue — it's a documented institutional practice with a dig schedule.

One I'd add in the same family, because it's the exact real-world instance of "Forestry plants trees for hulls cut in eighty years": **Ise Jingū**, rebuilt every twenty years for well over a millennium, with the timber forests managed on multi-century rotations to supply rebuildings nobody alive will attend. A polity that maintains a silvicultural rotation for a ship it will not sail has a real precedent, and the precedent is a religious one, which is probably the right note.

And the export's own frame deserves keeping verbatim in the notes: **a university is a calendar of saints that has forgotten it is one.** Endowed chairs, named lectures, plaques, buildings. The machinery of canonization is already installed and already running; the setting only changes what's written on it.
