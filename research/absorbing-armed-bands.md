# Absorbing Armed Bands

### How polities have taken in organized young men without being taken over

*Research memo, companion in register to `research/prepper-beekeeping.md` and `research/water-systems-under-failure.md`. Real-world facts only — not tied to any fictional setting's numbers.*

**Primed, not finished.** This memo is sanded to a clean surface for grafting. Sections are scoped so further research can be welded onto any one of them without disturbing the others, and **Section X names the joints deliberately left open.** Where a claim is thin, it says so in place rather than only in the confidence notes.

---

## I. Stating the problem so it has an answer

The instinct is to treat organized young men as a threat to be removed. That framing has no solution, because the capability does not evaporate when a polity declines it — **it relocates.** Bands that are dissolved become banditry; bands that are paid off go elsewhere and come back more experienced; bands that are refused a charter organize without one.

The question is therefore never *whether* they are organized. It is **who they are organized for, under what document, and who can revoke it.**

Three properties make a band absorbable, and all three are present in a fraternity:

1. **Internal hierarchy that already functions.** Somebody is obeyed. An absorbing institution does not have to manufacture discipline, only to point existing discipline at a task — which is far cheaper and is why this route keeps getting chosen.
2. **A membership boundary the members care about.** Belonging is already the reward, so status can substitute for wages. This is the mechanism that makes absorption affordable and is also what makes it durable enough to become dangerous.
3. **Corporate continuity across individuals.** The band outlives any member. That is what lets an institution contract with it — and what lets it outlive the arrangement's authors.

The last property is the whole tragedy in miniature, and Section IX returns to it.

## II. The menu, with failure modes

Every historical response falls into one of these, and each has a characteristic way of going wrong. They are not exclusive; most successful regimes ran several at once.

| Response | Mechanism | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Suppress and disband** | Prohibition, dissolution | Capability relocates outside the walls as banditry |
| **Pay to leave** | Subsidy for departure | Buys time; they train elsewhere and return stronger |
| **Charter and license** | Legal recognition, revocable in theory | Creates standing that is not revocable in practice |
| **Contract per task** | Written engagement, fixed term | Price ratchets at each renewal; the contractor learns your weaknesses |
| **Absorb as permanent salaried body** | Payroll, barracks, uniform | Most stable, most expensive; the body eventually captures the state |
| **Cross-cut membership** | Mixed recruitment so no unit is coherent | Erodes as recruitment localizes over generations |
| **Maintain plurality** | Many small rivals rather than one large body | Fails on consolidation — see Section VIII |

The single most useful primary source on the dilemma is **Pliny, *Letters* 10.33–34.** Pliny, governing Bithynia, asks Trajan for permission to form a *collegium* of builders to fight fires after Nicomedia burns. Trajan refuses: such societies have troubled that province, whatever they are called, and it is better to rely on the equipment and on ordering the crowd to help. **An emperor declining to charter a fire brigade on the grounds that organized men become factions** is the problem stated with unusual candour by someone with every reason to want the fire put out.

## III. The university precedent: student nations

This is the closest available case, because the absorbing institution is a university and the absorbed bodies are its own young men.

**The nations were the original violent student organizations.** At Bologna the students' own *universitas* — divided into *citramontani* and *ultramontani*, subdivided by region of origin — hired and fined its professors and could shut the studium by leaving. At Paris the Faculty of Arts organized into four nations, each with an elected proctor. These were coherent, armed, geographically-based sworn associations of young men, and town-and-gown violence was chronic rather than exceptional.

**Absorption took the form of constitutional standing.** The mechanism was *privilegium fori* — scholars answerable to university or ecclesiastical courts rather than to the town's. That single provision converts a gang into an estate: it does not reduce the violence, it relocates jurisdiction over it.

Two events show the pattern and its price:

- **Paris, 1229.** A Carnival tavern brawl in the Bourg-Saint-Marcel escalated; troops acting under the queen regent killed students. Masters and students **dispersed from Paris for two years** — the strike weapon working exactly as designed. The settlement was Gregory IX's bull ***Parens scientiarum*** (1231), which confirmed the university's privileges and its right to suspend lectures. The riot produced a charter.
- **Oxford, St Scholastica Day, 10 February 1355.** A dispute over wine at the Swindlestock Tavern became two days of rioting; the figures usually reported are around sixty-three scholars and some thirty townspeople killed. The outcome was that **the university gained authority over the town**, and the mayor and bailiffs were required to attend an annual Mass and pay a penny for each dead scholar — a penance performed until 1825, roughly four hundred and seventy years.

**The finding, stated plainly: absorption works, and the bill goes to whoever is outside the charter.** In both cases the violence increased the corporation's power and diminished the town's. A polity that charters its young men is making a decision about the people those men are violent *toward*, not about the violence.

## IV. Roman practice: licensing as the control surface

Rome ran the full menu across three centuries and left the clearest record of switching between options.

**Suppression.** A senatorial decree of 64 BCE dissolved most *collegia*. Clodius's law of 58 BCE restored the *collegia compitalicia* — the neighbourhood associations — and they immediately became the organizational base of his street violence. Caesar dissolved the collegia again, excepting the ancient ones (Suetonius, *Divus Julius* 42). The oscillation is itself evidence that neither prohibition nor permission was stable alone.

**Licensing as the settled regime.** The Augustan solution, generally placed in the late first century BCE as the *lex Julia de collegiis*, required associations to be authorized by the Senate or emperor. This is the durable answer Rome arrived at: not prohibition, not laissez-faire, but **a permission that is individually granted and therefore individually withdrawable** — at least on paper.

**Converting a private capability into a public service.** Augustus established the *vigiles* in 6 CE: seven cohorts, some seven thousand men, initially freedmen, salaried, tasked with fire watch and night policing. This is the cleanest historical instance of taking a dangerous or extortionate private function and making it a paid public body. Plutarch's *Crassus* supplies the standard backstory that Crassus profited from fires by buying burning property cheap; the further claim that he ran a fire brigade as an extortion racket is a popular elaboration rather than something the source states, and should be treated accordingly.

**Chartered youth bodies.** *Collegia iuvenum* — municipal associations of young men engaged in games, hunting and paramilitary display — are attested epigraphically across Italy and look very much like licensed youth corps. **This is the most directly analogous Roman institution and also the thinnest evidence base in this memo:** it is inscription-derived, the functions are inferred, and scholarly interpretation varies on how militarized they actually were. Flagged as a graft point in Section X.

## V. The contract path: condottieri

The Italian *condotta* — a written contract with a captain specifying pay, term, troop numbers and obligations — is the most fully developed version of per-task absorption, and it demonstrates both what contracting solves and what it does not.

**What it solved.** After the Peace of Brétigny (1360) demobilized companies ravaged France, and the practical response was to **export the problem**: Du Guesclin led routiers into the Castilian civil war. That is the pay-them-to-leave option at scale, and it worked in the sense that they left.

**What contracting achieved at its best.** John Hawkwood, captain of the White Company, ended his career as a salaried Florentine officer with a state funeral and a memorial in the Duomo. A dangerous captain converted into an honoured public official is the success case, and it was bought with money, status, and permanence.

**What it cost.** **Francesco Sforza was contracted as Milan's condottiere and took Milan in 1450**, founding a ducal dynasty. This is the near-term risk of the contract path in one name: the man you hire because he is competent and well-liked is precisely the man who can take the thing he was hired to defend.

**The control mechanism worth stealing.** Venice attached civilian commissioners — *provveditori* — to its armies, and rotated contracts among rival captains rather than letting any one become indispensable. **Embedded civilian oversight plus deliberate non-indispensability** is the most transferable administrative answer in this memo.

## VI. The permanent-body path: Janissaries and Mamluks

The most stable option, and the one whose failure is most instructive, because the failure is *designed against and happens anyway*.

**The Janissaries were built specifically to be incapable of becoming a dynasty.** Recruited by *devşirme* — a levy of Christian boys, largely from the Balkans, converted and trained — they were deliberately taken from distant provinces so as to have no local kin network (the cross-cutting mechanism of Section II). They were forbidden to marry, to own property, or to practise a trade, and they lived in barracks. Every one of those provisions is an anti-capture device.

**All of them eroded.** From the late sixteenth century membership became effectively hereditary, pay tickets were bought and sold, and the corps acquired property and trades. It then became politically decisive: it deposed and killed Osman II in 1622, resisted the *Nizam-ı Cedid* reforms, and forced Selim III's deposition in 1807. Mahmud II destroyed the corps outright in 1826 — the *Vaka-i Hayriye*, the "Auspicious Incident." **A four-century arrangement ended by massacre because it could not be reformed.**

**The Mamluks ran the same arc faster and further:** slave soldiers who took power in Egypt in 1250 and ruled until 1517, and who remained a political force until Muhammad Ali destroyed them in 1811.

**The transferable finding is generational, not moral.** Nobody defected. The provisions were not repealed by a bad sultan. They decayed because each cohort of successors inherited the institution's capability without inheriting the founders' understanding of why the constraints existed. Constraints that depend on continuous enforcement are spent, slowly, by ordinary succession.

## VII. Labor as the absorption: corvée and the CCC

The route the water emergency actually takes, and it has both an ancient and a modern form.

**Corvée** — unpaid labor owed to a lord or state, used for roads, canals and fortification across ancient Egypt, ancien-régime France (the *corvée royale* for roads, abolished 1787), Qing China and colonial administrations. Labor as tax. Its characteristic failure is that it is extractive and resented, and it produces evasion rather than loyalty: it takes the men's time without engaging the thing that makes a band absorbable, namely its own hierarchy and status economy.

**The Civilian Conservation Corps (1933–1942)** is the modern and far more relevant case. Roughly three million young men enrolled over its life; camps were administered by the US Army while the work was directed by Agriculture and Interior; enrollees received thirty dollars a month with most of it remitted to their families.

**The part usually left out of the story is that it functioned as a security policy.** The CCC arrived immediately after the Bonus Army — organized, unemployed, militarily experienced men encamped on Washington and violently cleared in 1932. When a second Bonus March came in 1933, Roosevelt's response was to open CCC enrollment to them. **An organized body that had marched on the capital was absorbed by being offered work**, and it worked. That the CCC is remembered as conservation policy rather than as the management of dangerous men is itself worth noticing.

**What labor absorption buys and what it costs.** It buys the cheapest possible use of an existing hierarchy, and status paid in usefulness rather than cash. It costs **precedent**: after the first mobilization there is a capability, after the second an expectation, and eventually the institution cannot perform infrastructure work without them — at which point they hold a veto they never had to seize. Necessity is leverage, and it accrues without anyone deciding to acquire it.

## VIII. The consolidation problem

If the absorbed bodies are plural and rivalrous, **plurality is itself the safety mechanism**, and it is doing more work than any charter provision. A dozen groups that dislike each other cannot act in concert, cannot present a single negotiator, and cannot strike together. The institution's real security interest is therefore not the suppression of violence but **the prevention of merger.**

The cautionary case is exact. The **Praetorian Guard** was a set of cohorts until Sejanus concentrated them in a single barracks — the *castra praetoria* — under Tiberius in 23 CE. Physical consolidation converted a bodyguard into the body that made and unmade emperors, and the problem was only ended when Constantine disbanded it in 312. **The decisive act was administrative and looked like efficiency:** putting them in one place, under one command, for convenience.

Which means the dangerous proposal in any Senate is the reasonable one. Consolidation is always argued for on grounds of coordination, standardization, accountability, a single point of contact, fairness in the allocation of work. Every one of those arguments is correct. Rome's licensing regime deliberately chartered many small associations rather than a few large ones; Venice rotated contracts among rivals; the Ottomans mixed provinces of origin. **All three are versions of refusing to let the convenient thing happen.**

Corollaries worth having:

- A single spokesman is more dangerous than a single barracks, and cheaper to create.
- Standardizing terms across groups is a merger performed by paperwork.
- The moment one band becomes best at the work, plurality is already gone in substance.

## IX. Is there a hard cap?

Following the structure of the companion memos: is there a limit no amount of institutional skill can exceed?

Yes, and it is not material. **No absorption mechanism survives its own success.**

Each option in Section II works by making the absorbed body more useful. Usefulness makes it more capable, and being relied upon makes it necessary. **Capability plus necessity is leverage**, and leverage accrues whether or not anyone seeks it. The Janissaries did not conspire their way to deciding successions; they became the thing the state could not do without, and the rest followed. Sforza did not need to betray Milan so much as to notice what he already held.

The only variable is the rate. Mechanisms that slow it are all known and all the same in character: plurality, rotation, cross-cut recruitment, embedded civilian oversight, fixed terms, and constraints on property and inheritance. Every one of them is **an ongoing expense of attention rather than a device that stays installed**, which is why they are reliably the first things to lapse, and why they lapse by ordinary succession rather than by anyone's decision.

If a single sentence is wanted: **an armed band can be absorbed but not disposed of, and every arrangement that works converts a present danger into a future creditor.**

A second near-cap, on the Section III pattern: **absorption relocates the violence rather than reducing it.** Chartering the young men decides who they are dangerous toward. A polity that does this has chosen its own peace at the cost of whoever stands outside the charter, and the historical record is consistent that the outside party pays.

## X. Joints left open for grafting

Named deliberately, so further research can be welded on without re-cutting what is here.

1. **Fraternities and student societies specifically, 19th–21st century.** This memo reaches for medieval nations and stops. The actual institutional history — the German *Burschenschaften* and the *Mensur*, the American fraternity system's periodic suppressions and readmissions, university disciplinary regimes and their failures — is a whole section and is the nearest analog of all.
2. **The *collegia iuvenum* evidence base.** Flagged thin in Section IV. Wants the epigraphic scholarship handled properly rather than summarized.
3. **Sworn brotherhoods outside Europe.** Chinese sworn-brotherhood and secret-society traditions, Japanese *ikki* and village leagues, West African age-grade societies — the last especially, since age-grade systems are formal, universal, and explicitly designed to absorb every young man of a cohort into graded public duties.
4. **Guild and militia overlap.** In many towns the guild *was* the militia. Where the labor body and the armed body are the same institution by design, the absorption question is structured entirely differently, and that is close to what a mobilized fraternity actually is.
5. **Demobilization as its own literature.** The systematic modern study of what happens to organized fighters after a conflict ends — disarmament, demobilization and reintegration — is directly on point and entirely absent here.
6. **The economics.** What absorption cost as a fraction of revenue, in any of these cases. Not attempted; would materially change which options look available to a poor polity.
7. **Women's position in the absorbed body and in the polity that absorbs it.** Every case above is male by construction, and the memo has not asked what that does to the institution doing the absorbing.
8. **Failure cases where absorption was refused and nothing bad happened.** The memo is built from cases that became notable, which selects for drama. A serious version needs the null results.

## Confidence notes

- **Solid primary sources, directly citable:** Pliny, *Letters* 10.33–34 (Trajan refusing a fire-brigade *collegium* at Nicomedia); Suetonius, *Divus Julius* 42 (Caesar dissolving collegia except ancient ones); Gregory IX, *Parens scientiarum* (1231).
- **Solid, standard historical consensus:** the 64 BCE senatorial dissolution of collegia and Clodius's 58 BCE restoration of the *collegia compitalicia* as a base for street violence; Augustan licensing of associations as the settled imperial regime; the *vigiles* founded 6 CE at seven cohorts of freedmen; the Praetorian Guard concentrated in the *castra praetoria* under Tiberius in 23 CE and disbanded by Constantine in 312; *privilegium fori* as the constitutional basis of scholarly immunity; the Paris dispersion of 1229–31; the *condotta* system; Hawkwood's Florentine service and Duomo memorial; Francesco Sforza taking Milan in 1450; Venetian *provveditori*; *devşirme* recruitment and the original Janissary constraints on marriage, property and trade, their erosion from the late 16th century, the depositions of Osman II (1622) and Selim III (1807), and the corps' destruction in 1826; Mamluk rule in Egypt 1250–1517 and their destruction in 1811; the *corvée royale* abolished 1787; CCC dates, approximate three million enrollees, Army camp administration, and $30/month pay largely remitted to families.
- **Widely reported, figures conventional rather than firmly established:** the St Scholastica Day casualty figures of roughly 63 scholars and 30 townspeople, and the annual penance running to 1825. These numbers are repeated consistently but derive from a thin documentary base; treat as the received account.
- **Attributed but weaker than usually presented:** Crassus profiting from fires comes from Plutarch's *Crassus*; the further claim that he operated a fire brigade as an extortion racket is a later popular elaboration and is not what the source says.
- **Thin evidence base, flagged in place:** the *collegia iuvenum* as licensed paramilitary youth corps. Attested epigraphically, but function and degree of militarization are inferred and contested. See Section X.2.
- **My synthesis, not any source's framing:** reading the CCC as a security response to the Bonus Army. The underlying facts are solid — the 1932 clearing of the Bonus Army, the 1933 second march, and Roosevelt opening CCC enrollment to the marchers — but presenting the CCC primarily as the management of organized men is an interpretation, not the standard historiography.
- **Also my synthesis:** the Section IX claim that no absorption mechanism survives its own success, and the Section VIII claim that consolidation is the true security variable. Both are assembled from the cases above rather than drawn from a source that argues them. The individual cases are sound; the generalizations are mine and should be argued with.
