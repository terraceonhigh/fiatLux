# Trapping and Small Game

### Running a line with no resupply, and why lean meat alone kills you

*Research memo, companion in register to `research/prepper-beekeeping.md`, `research/water-systems-under-failure.md` and `research/absorbing-armed-bands.md`. Real-world facts only — not tied to any fictional setting's numbers.*

**Primed, not finished.** Sanded for grafting: sections are scoped so further research welds onto any one without disturbing the others, thin claims are flagged in place, and **Section VIII names the joints left open.**

---

## I. The trap type is the whole design decision

Under permanent no-resupply the question is not which trap catches best but **which trap a local economy can keep making.** The four families sort cleanly, and they sort against each other:

| Family | Manufactured input | Reusability | Sustainability verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deadfalls** | None — wood, stone, cordage | Rebuilt each time | Never runs out; lowest yield per hour |
| **Snares** | Drawn wire or cable, a lock | High, until the wire work-hardens | Best ratio of yield to input |
| **Footholds** | Tempered spring steel, chain, swivels | Decades with maintenance | Blacksmith-repairable; springs are the weak point |
| **Body-grippers** | Precision springs, formed jaws | Decades | Hardest to reproduce; most dangerous |

**Spring steel is the chokepoint for everything but the deadfall.** Coil and longspring traps depend on steel tempered to hold a spring rate through thousands of cycles and years of wet. A competent smith can forge and temper springs; doing it *consistently* is a different skill, and a spring that has lost temper closes weakly, which is worse than not closing — it means wounded animals that pull free.

**Snares are the sustainable workhorse.** A snare is a loop of cable or wire with a lock that lets it close and not reopen. It is light enough to carry fifty of, cheap enough to lose, and simple enough that the failure mode is a kinked cable rather than a mechanism. Wire eventually work-hardens at the bends and snaps, so the consumable is wire itself, which needs a drawing capability — a real dependency, but a far shallower one than tempered springs.

**Deadfalls are the floor nobody falls through.** A figure-four or Paiute deadfall is sticks, cordage and a rock. The catch: they are site-specific, weather-sensitive, must be rebuilt after every firing, and **are genuinely dangerous to set,** because setting one means holding a heavy weight up with a structure designed to collapse. They are the answer when there is nothing, not the answer when there is anything.

## II. What each type does to a person

Relevant because a trapper's own line is the most likely thing to injure them.

**Footholds** are restraining traps: paired jaws driven by leg or coil springs, holding rather than killing. Modern welfare designs use offset or padded jaws and are rated by jaw spread. On a human foot or hand the injury is **crushing and degloving** — soft tissue and small bones — rather than amputation at the moment of closure. A trap strong enough for a large canid, closing on a foot, in boots, does not shear. It crushes.

**Body-gripping traps** — the rotating-jaw design commercially associated with Frank Conibear from the 1950s, sized roughly 110/120 for marten and mink through 220 for raccoon to 330 for beaver — kill by striking the spine or skull. **These are the traps that maim people and dogs.** The large sizes carry enough force to break fingers and wrists, which is why setting them requires setting tongs and why the safety catches exist. A 330 is not a thing you free yourself from one-handed.

**Snares** injure by constriction. On a limb, a cable lock under load causes deep tissue and vascular damage rather than fracture.

**The amputation pathway is usually not the trap.** In a setting without prompt surgical care, the sequence that costs a foot is: crushing injury with devitalised tissue and contaminated wound → infection → progressive tissue loss → amputation at a level above the damage. A mangled foot goes to a **transtibial (below-knee) amputation**, because the surgical level must be through viable tissue and the foot is what the trap ruined. That yields a below-knee stump and a prosthesis bearing on the residual limb.

Worth stating plainly because it reverses the intuition: **the trap causes the injury; the absence of care causes the loss.** These are separable events, often weeks apart.

## III. The line as a discipline

A trapline is not a set of devices, it is a **maintained circuit**, and almost everything that goes wrong is a lapse of discipline rather than of equipment.

**Check intervals are the core obligation.** Jurisdictions commonly mandate checks between 24 and 72 hours depending on trap type — restraining sets more frequently than killing sets — and the underlying reasons hold regardless of law: a held animal suffers, a dead animal spoils, and a caught animal is a baited set for something else. Scavengers find your catch before you do. **A line you cannot check on schedule is a line that is too long**, and the constraint is not how many traps you own but how far you can walk in a day, repeatedly, in weather.

**Sets are not marked, so the line lives in memory.** Marking makes sets findable — by thieves, by anyone curious about who lives back there. So the map is carried in the head, refreshed by walking it. **A trap you have forgotten is the dangerous one**, both because it holds something indefinitely and because it is still armed where you no longer expect it. This is the trapper's characteristic anxiety and it is administrative rather than dramatic: an inventory you can never fully verify.

**Scent and rust discipline.** New or rusty traps are boiled — traditionally in a bath dyed with logwood chips or walnut hulls — to strip oil and manufacturing scent and to blacken the steel against corrosion, then **waxed**, which both slows rust and speeds the action. Handling is done with clean gloves. This is a seasonal maintenance loop, not a one-off.

**Freeze-up is the winter failure mode.** Moisture in the pan and under the jaws freezes the mechanism solid. Countermeasures are waxed traps, bedding in dry peat or sifted dry dirt rather than damp soil, and pan covers. A frozen line is invisible failure: the circuit looks fine and catches nothing.

**Set types, since the vocabulary is precise.** A **baited set** uses food; a **lure set** uses scent; a **cubby** or box set constrains the animal's approach so it must present correctly. A **blind set** uses no bait or lure at all — placed on a trail or run, relying entirely on knowing where the animal will walk. The blind set is the one that requires the most knowledge and leaves the least evidence.

## IV. What the line actually catches

**Hares and rabbits** travel established runs and are taken with snares set on those runs at head height for the species — a snare set anywhere else catches nothing. This is the highest-volume small game in most temperate and boreal settings and the foundation of Section V's problem.

**The supply is not stable, and the instability is long.** Snowshoe hare populations cycle with an amplitude of an order of magnitude or more on a period of roughly **eight to eleven years**, the classic cycle visible in Hudson's Bay Company fur returns and studied experimentally at Kluane, where Krebs and colleagues showed that food and predation together drive it rather than either alone (Krebs, Boutin, Boonstra, Sinclair, Smith, Dale, Martin & Turkington, "Impact of Food and Predation on the Snowshoe Hare Cycle," *Science*, 1995, vol. 269, pp. 1112–1115). **A subsistence trapper cannot plan on yield**, and the crash interval is longer than most people's working memory of the last one.

**Beaver and muskrat** are taken in water sets. Beaver matter disproportionately for reasons that are nutritional rather than culinary — see below.

**Squirrels** are taken on pole sets; **grouse and ptarmigan** with light snares, sometimes on a leaning-pole arrangement. (The specific "Ojibwa bird pole" widely described in bushcraft literature has thinner ethnographic provenance than its confident presentation suggests; flagged as a graft point.)

**Deer are shot, not trapped.** Body weight and power defeat foothold traps and no ethical or practical trap scales to them. Large-game protein therefore depends on a firearm and its ammunition, which is a separate dependency chain entirely.

## V. Rabbit starvation: the arithmetic that kills

This is the section that matters most, because it is counterintuitive and lethal, and because it turns two apparently domestic activities into survival infrastructure.

**The mechanism.** The liver's capacity to deaminate amino acids and the urea cycle's capacity to dispose of the resulting nitrogen are both finite. Beyond a ceiling, protein intake produces hyperammonemia and elevated blood urea, presenting as nausea, diarrhoea, lassitude and — if it continues without fat or carbohydrate — death, typically within weeks. The ceiling is commonly placed at roughly **35% of total energy from protein**, or on the order of 2–2.5 g per kg body weight per day (reviewed in Bilsborough & Mann, "A review of issues of dietary protein intake in humans," *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism*, 2006, vol. 16, pp. 129–152). The condition is known as **rabbit starvation**, popularised by Vilhjalmur Stefansson from Arctic experience, and as ***mal de caribou*** in French-Canadian usage.

**Why hares specifically.** Wild rabbit and hare are exceptionally lean — body fat on the order of a few percent, and lower still in late winter. A person eating nothing but hare can eat continuously, feel full, and starve, because virtually all of the available energy is arriving as the one macronutrient with a hard disposal ceiling. Eating *more* hare makes it worse rather than better.

**So the trapline is only two-thirds of a diet.** The missing pieces are fat and carbohydrate, and both have to come from somewhere else:

- **Fat.** Beaver were historically prized for it, the tail especially; waterfowl carry substantial subcutaneous fat; bone marrow and organ fat are worth the labour of extraction. Rendered fat stores well and is the single most valuable trade good a trapper produces after hides. (A general caution worth knowing: the livers of some large carnivores and marine mammals carry enough vitamin A to be acutely toxic — polar bear liver is the documented case. Organ meat is valuable and not indiscriminately so.)
- **Carbohydrate.** Berries, roots, honey. Preserves.

**Late winter is the pinch.** Animals are leanest at the end of winter, exactly when stored fat and preserves are most depleted — the classic spring famine. A trapper's year is not a flat line with a hungry season; it is a race to bank fat and sugar before the animals themselves run out of fat.

## VI. Preservation, and what jam is actually doing

**Sugar preserves by denying water, not by killing.** A jam is shelf-stable at roughly **65% soluble solids** (about 65 °Brix), at which water activity is low enough to prevent microbial growth. Under-concentrated jam ferments or moulds; over-concentrated jam crystallises.

**The gel needs three things in balance:** pectin, acid and sugar. Wild huckleberries and blueberries are relatively **low in pectin**, so they set poorly on their own and want either added pectin, a high-pectin fruit such as crabapple, or prolonged boiling — which costs fuel and darkens the result.

**Honey as the sweetener works and behaves differently.** Honey is roughly 80% sugars and 17–18% water, so it contributes moisture that must be boiled off, it is mildly acidic, and it browns and dominates flavour. Honey-sweetened preserves are traditional and entirely viable; they are not a drop-in substitute for refined sugar by weight.

**Safety sits on acidity.** *Clostridium botulinum* does not grow below about pH 4.6, and fruit jams sit comfortably below that, which is why high-sugar high-acid preserves are among the safest home-preserved foods. Low-acid foods are the dangerous category, and the distinction is worth keeping straight: a jam is safe for reasons a preserved vegetable is not.

**And the synthesis is a single food.** Dried lean meat, pounded, mixed with rendered fat, often with dried berries worked in — **pemmican** — is lean protein, fat and carbohydrate combined in one keeping ration, at roughly equal weights of dried meat and fat. It is the direct answer to Section V, it stores for years, and it is exactly the three things a trapline, a fat cache and a berry patch produce. A trapper who makes preserves and renders fat is not diversifying into domesticity; those are the two halves of what makes the meat survivable.

## VII. Is there a hard cap?

Following the companion memos: is there a limit no amount of skill exceeds?

**Not in the equipment.** Deadfalls need nothing manufactured, so the *capability* to trap never disappears. What degrades is **efficiency**: one person can run scores of snares or dozens of footholds, and perhaps a dozen deadfalls, because deadfalls must be rebuilt after each firing and cannot be left set through weather. Losing wire and springs does not end trapping; it collapses the number of sets one person can maintain, and therefore the calories.

**The cap is the animals, and it is a commons.** Trapping pressure on a local population compounds, and the historical record on this is unambiguous — the fur trade depleted beaver across entire regions within decades. A trapline's sustainable yield is set by a population its holder does not control and shares with everyone else working the same ground. One careful trapper cannot conserve a resource that several are drawing on.

**And the yield oscillates on a period longer than institutional memory.** With hares cycling on eight to eleven years, a trapper who begins in a peak learns the wrong lesson about how much country will support, and the correction arrives years later as a crash that looks like bad luck or bad technique rather than the return of a mean.

If a single sentence is wanted: **the equipment has no floor under it, but the yield is set by a cycling animal population held in common, so a trapline can be run skilfully and still fail for reasons entirely outside the trapper's practice.**

A second near-cap, on the pattern of the other memos: **the line is limited by one person's memory and one person's legs.** Sets are unmarked by necessity and checked on foot by obligation. That bounds the enterprise at whatever circuit can be walked reliably and recalled completely — and unlike wire or springs, that ceiling gets lower with age and injury, not higher with skill.

## VIII. Joints left open for grafting

1. **Regional specificity.** Species, seasons and habits for coastal and interior British Columbia. This memo is written at temperate/boreal generality and would want localising.
2. **Below-knee prosthetics.** Socket fabrication and fit, liners, volume fluctuation through the day, skin breakdown on a residual limb, and what maintenance a prosthesis needs with no clinic. Directly load-bearing and entirely absent here.
3. **Hides and tanning.** What a trapper actually trades, how it is processed, and what the finished goods are worth against food.
4. **Firearms and ammunition sustainability.** Reloading, primer manufacture, powder, barrel life. The one dependency chain a trapline cannot substitute for, since large game requires it.
5. **Trapline tenure as a property regime.** British Columbia registers traplines as areas with exclusive rights, transferable and inheritable. **A recognised trapline is a form of title**, which makes it directly relevant to any question of who counts as affiliated with what — a trapper may hold standing in one polity's records and none in another's.
6. **The "traditional trap" literature's provenance.** Much bushcraft writing attributes specific designs to specific peoples with more confidence than the ethnography supports. Wants doing properly rather than repeated.
7. **Fish and shellfish.** Weirs, traps, drying. Omitted entirely, and in a coastal setting probably a larger calorie source than the trapline.
8. **Dogs.** Working dogs change the economics of hunting and hauling substantially, and are also the animal most often killed by body-gripping traps.
9. **Winter travel.** Snowshoes, hauling a toboggan, and what a below-knee prosthesis does to all of it.

## Confidence notes

- **Solid, standard practice, verifiable in trapping and wildlife-management references:** the four trap families and their mechanisms; foothold traps as restraining rather than killing devices, with offset and padded welfare designs; rotating-jaw body-gripping traps sized roughly 110/120 through 220 to 330, killing by spinal or cranial strike, requiring setting tongs and safety catches; snare construction and the requirement to set on established runs; boiling and waxing traps for scent and corrosion control; dry bedding and pan covers against freeze-up; the set-type vocabulary including the blind set.
- **Solid, directly citable:** Krebs, Boutin, Boonstra, Sinclair, Smith, Dale, Martin & Turkington 1995 (*Science* 269:1112–1115) on food and predation jointly driving the snowshoe hare cycle; Bilsborough & Mann 2006 (*IJSNEM* 16:129–152) reviewing maximum protein intake. The pH 4.6 growth threshold for *C. botulinum* and the ~65 °Brix figure for shelf-stable jam are both standard food-science values.
- **Well established but range-dependent:** the 8–11 year hare cycle period and its order-of-magnitude amplitude; the ~35% of energy protein ceiling, which is a reviewed approximation rather than a fixed physiological constant and varies with body size, condition and adaptation; wild hare fat content "a few percent," which varies seasonally and by species; honey at ~80% sugars and 17–18% water; roughly equal weights of dried meat to fat in pemmican; trap check intervals of 24–72 hours, which are jurisdiction- and trap-type-specific.
- **Historically documented:** rabbit starvation and *mal de caribou* as recognised conditions, with Stefansson as the popularising source from Arctic experience; regional depletion of beaver by the fur trade; acute vitamin A toxicity from polar bear liver.
- **Thin provenance, flagged in place:** the Ojibwa bird pole and similar named "traditional" trap designs as presented in bushcraft literature. The traps work; the attributions are shakier than the sources imply. See Section VIII.6.
- **My synthesis, not any source's framing:** separating the trap injury from the amputation as two distinct events weeks apart, and the Section VII claim that the binding constraint is a commons-held cycling population rather than equipment. The clinical pathway (crush injury, contamination, infection, transtibial amputation) is ordinary trauma surgery; presenting it as *the absence of care rather than the trap* being the cause of loss is an interpretive emphasis. Likewise the reading of preserves and rendered fat as survival infrastructure rather than domestic activity follows from Section V but is not how either the trapping or the food-preservation literature frames it.
