# Absorbing the houses

### Research support for: the university takes the police station and the fraternities

*Research brief. Confidence notes at the end.*

> **How this was produced, because it changes how much weight it carries.** The chapter-two beat was derived in conversation, which meant it was contaminated by its own derivation. So the strategic problem was handed cold to **three independent analysts** with no knowledge of this project — no canon, no characters, no bible, and none of the conclusions already reached. Each got the same thin scenario (slow collapse thirty years in; a university that has absorbed civil functions without deciding to; no jail, no armed force, no experience of coercion; an unpaid police force holding the district's only cells; a dozen armed, propertied, self-selecting fraternity chapters selling protection to neighbours) and a different disciplinary lens: **comparative politics** (state formation and delegated coercion), a **security practitioner** (sequencing and asset custody), and a **historian of universities and student bodies**.
>
> They agreed on more than expected, and by three different routes rather than by citing one shared precedent — which is the stronger form of agreement. Where this brief says *unanimous*, it means three analysts who could not see each other's work.

---

## The rules all three arrived at

These are the load-bearing findings. Anything the Academy does that violates one of them is a decision to fail in a way three specialists could see coming.

**1. Never charter a house as a unit. Enlist individuals.** Unanimous, and the reasoning was identical each time: bloc incorporation ratifies the existing chain of command. You acquire a flag and the chapter president acquires a legal army. The men must be sworn one at a time, by name, onto a roll the institution holds.

**2. Every benefit attaches to the named man and the dependents he names — never to a house account.** Housing, rations, heat, clinic access, tuition, credential. The moment any of it is issued to a house, individual enlistment is over.

**3. Take the police assets physically, first, and say nothing to the houses while you do it.** Cells, evidence locker, radio net, criminal records, vehicles. Unanimous, and the practitioner put it in week one. The framing that stuck: unpaid sworn officers holding the district's coercive infrastructure are *not a force, they are an inventory awaiting a claimant*, and there is exactly one other buyer.

**4. Buy the oath, not the men.** The police hold the one thing that cannot be improvised — sworn legal continuity. Pay them in housing, heat, hospital access and tuition for their children, in exchange for the swearing-in power and the records, and write down while they are still alive the rule by which the senior sworn officer swears the next generation. A thin lawful thread is worth more than a thick unlawful one, because it is the thing the houses cannot mint either.

**5. Rotation, and no man polices his own street.** Unanimous. Crews are never single-house; no constable serves the district containing his own chapter; terms are fixed; rosters are drawn by the registrar and never submitted by the houses. The practitioner called the mixed-crew clause the most important operational sentence in the whole instrument, because it is what breaks protection-racket geography.

**6. Do not attempt disarmament.** You cannot enforce it, and attempting it is the single move that unites twelve rival houses. Hold the register instead — **custody can be conceded, the ledger cannot.** Rounds issued and returned, counted per shift, discrepancy triggers a report.

**7. The tribunal must never acquire custodial power.** Every sanction is an exclusion — ration card, heat, clinic access, enrolment, credential, removal from the roll — and none is confinement. The moment the board can jail people, it becomes the thing worth capturing. The university takes the cells; the tribunal must never own them.

**8. Open the roll to non-members on identical terms**, or the chapters remain the only route to armed status and the licence becomes theirs rather than yours.

**9. The credential is the currency, and it must be held in a man's own name.** The university is the only mint left. What the houses are currently buying with armed patronage — deference, precedence, a claim to be the district's first men — the Academy can issue directly and more cheaply, and can revoke.

## The failure mode, predicted three times out of three

**Not a coup. Horizontal capture.**

- *Politics:* the design works, the chapters dissolve into the roll, and then the constabulary reconstitutes exactly what was dissolved — age-grading, initiation, an alumni network, an economic base — and in fifteen years the institution faces the same problem with a body that has uniforms, legal standing and the keys.
- *Practitioner:* **federation.** The twelve begin bargaining as one, and the renewal cycle inverts.
- *Historian:* **fusion at the bottom, by administrative convenience rather than by coup.** Rotation is unpopular and inefficient, clerks are overworked, the houses offer to save everyone time.

**This independently confirms §VI Phase 5** — *first serious coup attempt, from Facilities or the militia; the absorbed garrison does not believe a committee should command it.* Three analysts with no access to the bible predicted the garrison the bible already contains. The absorption written in decade three is the origin of the Phase 5 attempt, and the polity has roughly a generation and a half between them.

### The renewal trap

Once the houses federate, non-renewal of all twelve stops being a credible act. Every renewal cycle therefore becomes a negotiation the institution loses slightly, on schedule, forever. **Tribute with a review date.**

### And the sanction schedule dies at the first flinch

A productive constable holding a difficult ward does something serious. The panel is told that removing him leaves the ward unheld. It suspends instead of expelling. Everyone on the roll learns the real price of the top sanction inside a week, and after that the code is advisory. This is the near-term failure and it arrives long before the federation does.

## The earliest indicator — and it is always a clerical convenience

All three were asked for the first observable sign of failure. All three answered with a labour-saving request that a competent clerk is right to grant:

- Men trading shifts until they are posted with their own chapter brothers, arriving at the desk as a scheduling complaint that the swap requests have become unmanageable.
- Crews swapping shifts without calling dispatch — indistinguishable from ordinary rota informality, and no duty officer will flag it.
- A house asking to be billed for its members' rations as a house account instead of per-man cards, *framed as a bookkeeping simplification, and the clerk who agrees will be right that it saves labour.*

Two of the three landed on shift-swapping specifically. Confirming signals, later and unmistakable: renewal applications filed in a batch in a common format; one alumni agent countersigning for more than one house; a house name appearing in the *responsible party* field of the clinic intake ledger; dependent registrations growing faster than the roll, with men declaring one another as dependents.

For §0's purposes this is a gift — the polity's turning points are a shift book, a ration ledger and an intake form, and the person who could see it is a scheduler nobody consults.

## Where they split — the shape of the vote

The one question that produced three coherent, incompatible answers. Useful precisely because §0 requires the losing side not to be cowards.

| Lens | Position |
|---|---|
| **Politics** | **Charter nothing.** Refuse in writing, publicly, to sign anything with a chapter as a body. Open the roll and let the houses bleed members one at a time. Tolerate the holdouts: unattacked, they cannot recruit on grievance, and *a house that cannot heat itself and cannot credential its members is not a rival government, it is a landlord with guns.* |
| **Practitioner** | **Charter one house at a time, hungriest first.** Ninety-day revocable licences, weakest chapter approached first so that once three have signed the licence has a market price and the strong houses face exclusion rather than negotiation. Never convene all twelve, on any subject, including trivial ones. |
| **Historian** | **Charter all twelve — for status, never for armed service.** Seals, a published order of precedence by date of foundation (the one criterion no house can contest and every house has boasted about for a century), and seats in Convocation. Armed service remains individual and sworn. Rivalry becomes a race to be first-chartered rather than a reason to resist. |

Note that the historian independently invented **student co-governance** as the instrument — seats for the houses in the governing body — without any knowledge that this is the professor's own field.

## The hole in the plan

**The alumni corporations are what federate the twelve, and nobody in the room will see it.**

They are older, wealthier, outside the conduct code's jurisdiction entirely, already known to one another, and structurally invisible to a dispatch centre that only tracks men on shift. Every control in the design points at undergraduates; the brokers are their fathers' generation, and they hold the deeds.

Only one of the three saw it. It is the strongest available argument for the losing side of the vote, and it does not require the opponent to be wrong about anything else.

The corollary instrument, if the Academy wants a grip: make the alumni corporation **countersign each charter and pledge house property as forfeit bond.** They will resist it, and it is worth the fight — their title is only real because the Academy's registry says so, and property is the one asset they cannot hide or move. That also converts the vital-records office into a security instrument, which is the cheapest coercion available to a polity that cannot fight: *recognition of title is how you get a claim on the protection market without fielding a gun.*

## What it costs, permanently

Compiled from all three; none of it is recoverable.

- **The institution becomes a government and cannot resign.** Once it holds the custody register, walking away is itself an atrocity.
- **It owns the violence without commanding it.** It feeds the force, so every beating is the university's beating, and everyone will correctly say so.
- **The clinic's neutrality.** Keying subsistence and medical access to the roll makes medicine an instrument of governance; publishing a wound registry makes it an intelligence organ, and people begin dying at home rather than be logged.
- **Faculty governance in this domain, forever.** Power moves to the bursar and the registrar, because a payroll captures its master.
- **The conduct code stops being educational.** It is a criminal court, and within a few years it will be asked to do things a conduct code should never do, and it will do them.
- **Admission becomes a security instrument**, so the academic mission is downstream of the security calculus rather than the reverse.
- **A standing claim on housing, heat, food and hospital capacity that competes directly with students.**

---

## What this means for the chapter

**The plan is correct and it is obvious.** Three independent specialists converged on individual enlistment, rotation, registrar-held rosters, and seize-the-police-assets-first. So the drama cannot be *what will they decide* — a competent room reaches this answer.

What it can be: **every one of these designs predicts its own failure, all three predict the same failure, and the person proposing it knows the literature better than anyone in the room.** The opposition's strongest line is therefore not that the plan is wrong. It is that the plan builds the garrison that refuses the Senate in fifteen years, and names the date.

The answer to that is: fifteen years, against next winter.

## Confidence notes

**Solid, and safe to build on:** the Janissaries abolished 1826 after acquiring hereditary interest and an independent economic base; the Ulster Special Constabulary formed 1920 substantially from existing Ulster Volunteer Force personnel; Vienna's Akademische Legion crushed by Windischgrätz in October 1848; *tuanlian* gentry militia professionalised into Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army in the 1850s, with military weight moving permanently to provincial notables; the Karlsbad Decrees of 1819 following Sand's assassination of Kotzebue; the Kamajor hunting societies mobilised into Sierra Leone's Civil Defence Forces, with the initiation apparatus proving an efficient cohesion mechanism and commanders later prosecuted; Gambetta, *The Sicilian Mafia: The Business of Private Protection* (1993) on protection as a commodity sold where a credible guarantor is absent, and Tilly's "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" (1985); the 1229 Paris *cessatio* settled by *Parens scientiarum* in 1231; the St Scholastica Day riot of 10 February 1355, whose settlement favoured the university and whose civic penance ran to 1825.

**Newly pinned.** The Cambridge proctors' power to detain women in the Spinning House was removed **by Act of Parliament in 1894**, after the Daisy Hopkins case, with the power passing to local magistrates. Oxford maintained its own university police force from 1829 until the University Council disbanded it in 2003. Both verified during the panel; this supersedes the looser "sometime in the 1890s" used earlier.

**Arc reliable, details loose — do not quote figures:**

- **Colombia's Convivir.** Legally authorised private security cooperatives that became a recruitment and legitimation pipeline into the paramilitary consolidation. The closest available analogue, because it began as *lawful protection-selling by locally rooted armed groups* — which is precisely the fraternities' current position. Decree 356 of 1994 and a Constitutional Court restriction in 1997 is close to right; treat the legal mechanics as unverified.
- **Sons of Iraq / Sahwa, roughly 2006–08.** Fighters enrolled and paid *individually* rather than through their commanders; violence fell while payments continued and the programme failed at transition, because the state that was to absorb the men largely did not. This is the case both for individual enlistment and for making the reward a permanent credentialled career rather than a stipend. Headcounts and absorption percentages were held loosely by the source and should not be repeated as fact. Note the disanalogy: that programme paid cash, which the Academy cannot. The transferable lesson is the *individual addressing* of the benefit, not the medium.
- **Nineteenth-century American state-level fraternity bans** producing sub-rosa chapters rather than dissolution: the pattern is real, the specific states and dates were not verified.

**Bibliographic.** The North American Interfraternity Conference was founded 27 November 1909 in New York. Chapter houses being held by alumni corporations legally distinct from the university is standard, not an invention of this setting.
