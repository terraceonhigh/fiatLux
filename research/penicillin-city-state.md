# Holding the Line at Penicillin

### Research notes: how a university survives the end of the state as a pharmaceutical polity

*Research brief, not prose. Confidence notes at the end — the load-bearing numbers are flagged.*

---

## The short answer

It is possible, it has been done under something close to these conditions, and it is precarious in ways that are much more interesting than the technology itself.

Oxford in 1940–41 is the proof of concept. Howard Florey's team at the Dunn School produced the world's first clinically useful penicillin in a university building, using bedpans as culture vessels, an extraction rig Norman Heatley assembled from milk churns, copper tubing and a repurposed doorbell, and a workforce of six young women — the "penicillin girls" — hand-tending mould cultures. Yields were so poor that they recovered unmetabolised penicillin from their patients' urine and re-administered it. When they ran out mid-course on their first patient, a policeman named Albert Alexander, he relapsed and died.

That last sentence is the whole setting in miniature. The bottleneck is never knowledge. It is throughput, continuity, and the political question of who gets the vial.

So the useful framing for fiction is not *can they* but: **what breaks first, how fast, and who owns the thing that breaks.**

# Part I — Penicillin as the index case

## The chain, ranked by how tight the chokepoint is

Tightest first. This ranking is where to apply narrative pressure.

### 1. The strain — the single irreplaceable asset

Wild *Penicillium* off a windowsill gives you a trace of penicillin and a great deal of disappointment. What made industrial penicillin possible was a specific mutant lineage: the cantaloupe isolate found in a Peoria market (*P. chrysogenum* NRRL 1951, collected by lab assistant Mary Hunt — "Moldy Mary"), then pushed through rounds of X-ray and UV mutagenesis and selection at Wisconsin into the Wis Q-series. Between the wild ancestor and the improved strains lie orders of magnitude of yield.

A university microbiology department plausibly holds descendants of that lineage as a lyophilised culture collection or on agar slants under mineral oil. This is the crown jewel, and it has three properties that are gifts to a writer:

- **It cannot be re-derived.** Not without decades of mutagenesis and screening, which means an X-ray source, a selection programme, and a bioassay pipeline run for a generation.
- **It degenerates.** High-yield industrial strains are unstable; serial subculture selects for revertants that grow faster and produce less. Keeping the strain productive means keeping *stocks* rather than cultures — lyophilised ampoules, silica-gel stocks, soil stocks, oil-overlaid slants — with a disciplined replacement schedule and periodic re-isolation of high-producing single-spore colonies against the assay.
- **It is a curatorial office.** Someone keeps the register, decides when an ampoule is opened, and certifies that what came out of it is still what went in.

**The Curator of the Collection is a political office**, whether the charter admits it or not. Loss of the collection is the setting's extinction event, and it happens not by dramatic sabotage but by a freezer failing over a long weekend, or by three careless generations of subculture.

### 2. Continuous power for agitation and aeration

Surface culture — mould mats on shallow trays of medium — needs almost no power and gives almost nothing. Deep-tank submerged fermentation is the whole ballgame, and it lives on air. A stirred, sparged tank needs a compressor and an agitator running without interruption for the length of a run (on the order of a week). Lose air for hours and the batch is a loss; lose it repeatedly and the plant is a rumour.

The relevant engineering is not capacity but **reliability**: micro-hydro on a mill race, producer gas from wood or coal running a stationary engine, a flywheel and belt shafting so mechanical drive survives loss of electrical generation. Redundancy is the design goal, not efficiency. A city-state with 40 kW that never stops beats one with 400 kW that stops weekly.

Air must also be *filtered* — cotton or glass-wool packed filters, sterilised with the vessel. An unfiltered sparge is a contamination lottery, and contamination in a deep tank is total loss.

### 3. Solvent supply and recovery

Penicillin is extracted by acidifying the filtered broth to roughly pH 2, pulling the free acid into amyl acetate or butyl acetate, then back-extracting into aqueous bicarbonate at neutral pH, and repeating to concentrate. It must be done **cold and fast**, because the beta-lactam ring hydrolyses in minutes at low pH and room temperature. Heatley's counter-current rig existed precisely to make the transfer fast.

Solvent can be made — acetic acid by fermentation and oxidation, amyl alcohol from fusel oil off a distillery, esterified with acid catalysis — but every cycle loses some to evaporation, entrainment and decomposition. This is the setting's **attritional bleed** rather than a cliff: not "the solvent ran out" but "recovery is down to 88% and the still needs new copper."

Buffers and reagents in the same category: sulfuric or phosphoric acid, sodium bicarbonate, activated carbon for decolourising. All makeable from limestone, brine, sulfur or pyrite, and charcoal — which is to say, all dependent on the hinterland.

### 4. Cold

Cold gates extraction, strain storage, biologicals, insulin, antitoxin, and the shelf life of everything aqueous. The period-appropriate answer that most worldbuilders miss: **absorption refrigeration runs on heat, not electricity.** An ammonia-water or lithium-bromide absorption cycle fired by wood, coal, waste heat or even solar can hold an ice-house and a cold room indefinitely with no moving parts to speak of. Pair it with cut lake ice and a well-insulated sawdust ice-house and you have a cold chain that does not care whether the generator is running.

An ice-house is also a wonderfully concrete thing for a polity to guard.

### 5. Pyrogen-free water, at volume

This is the quiet one, and it kills more people than a missing antibiotic. Bacterial endotoxin survives autoclaving. A drug can be sterile, potent and correctly dosed, and an injection made up in merely-boiled water will still spike a rigoring fever, and in a debilitated patient that can be fatal.

The answer is distillation with proper baffling to prevent droplet carry-over, glassware depyrogenated by dry heat at around 250 °C, and freshly-made solutions used within hours. This capability gates **every injectable in the pharmacopeia** — penicillin G, saline, antitoxin, insulin. A city-state that cannot make pyrogen-free water at volume is limited to oral drugs, which is a sharp and interesting constraint.

### 6. The assay standard

You cannot dose what you cannot measure, and a fermentation batch has no potency written on the side. The workhorse is the **cylinder-plate bioassay**: seed an agar plate with a sensitive test organism, place cylinders of sample and of a reference standard, incubate, measure the zones of inhibition, read potency off a dose-response line.

Two consequences worth using:

- It requires a **reference organism** kept as carefully as the production strain, and a **house working standard** — a batch of known potency held back and used to calibrate all later batches. That standard is itself perishable and must be periodically re-established from a new batch, and each re-establishment introduces drift.
- Over decades, the local unit therefore slides away from whatever the old international unit was. "Four hundred thousand Ashford units" is not quite four hundred thousand of anything anyone else recognises, and every trading partner knows it.

Weights and measures were always a sovereign function. Here the sovereign function is *potency*.

### 7. The consumables nobody can make

Salvage economy, slow decay, no substitute:

- **Rubber stoppers and gaskets.** Every closure, every pump seal. Reclaimed rubber degrades; natural rubber needs a climate the setting probably doesn't have.
- **Borosilicate glass.** Makeable in principle, hard in practice; needs boron ore and a glasshouse that can hold temperature. Breakage is the tax on everything.
- **Ball bearings, gauges, pH electrodes, stainless steel.** Bearings can be reground and races relined; a dead pH electrode sends you back to indicator dyes and titration, which is survivable but slower and coarser.
- **Fine filter media** — sintered glass, filter paper, cotton.

### 8. The medium — and why it ties you to the countryside

The Peoria breakthrough was not a machine, it was a waste stream: **corn steep liquor**, the sour by-product of wet-milling maize, added to the medium and multiplied yields substantially. Lactose or glucose as carbon source, corn steep for nitrogen and trace nutrients, chalk to buffer, and **phenylacetic acid** as a precursor to push the mould toward penicillin G rather than a mixture of congeners.

So the drug plant needs a wet mill, which needs a maize crop, which needs a hinterland that plants what the city asks for. This is the single most useful technical fact for the political half of the setting: **the antibiotic supply is agricultural.** A bad harvest is a drug shortage. Grain policy is health policy.

## The shortcut: penicillin V

This is the most valuable non-obvious fact in the brief.

Feed the fermentation **phenoxyacetic acid** instead of phenylacetic acid and the mould makes phenoxymethylpenicillin — penicillin V — instead of penicillin G. Penicillin V is **acid-stable**, which means it survives the stomach, which means it can be given **by mouth**.

For a resource-poor polity this is transformative, because it deletes the entire sterile-injectable apparatus at a stroke:

- no vials, no rubber stoppers, no needles, no syringes to sterilise
- no pyrogen-free water at volume
- no cold chain for the finished product — a dry powder or tablet in a sealed tin is stable for a long time at room temperature
- no trained injector; a runner can carry a course to a village three days away

Penicillin V is somewhat less potent than G against some organisms, and injectable penicillin remains necessary for severe sepsis, meningitis and endocarditis. But a city-state that can only make **oral** penicillin V is still a city-state that can cure most of what kills people in a pre-antibiotic world: strep throat and its rheumatic sequelae, cellulitis, dental abscess, syphilis, wound infection caught early.

Realistically the plant makes both: G for the hospital, V for the hinterland and the trade caravans. And that split is itself a political fault line — the injectable is the city's medicine, the tablet is the province's.

## Scale — the arithmetic that sets the story's altitude

One penicillin unit is 0.6 micrograms of penicillin G sodium, so a million units is about six-tenths of a gram. A serious infection wants millions of units a day for a week or more. Call it a few grams of pure drug per patient per course, and be generous, because a fifth to a third of what the tank produces will be lost in filtration, extraction and crystallisation.

Which gives three regimes, and they are three different polities:

| Regime | Method | Rough output | What it can treat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bedpan** | Surface culture in bottles, trays, whatever holds a litre | Grams a month | A handful of patients. Research, not medicine. This is Oxford 1941. |
| **Trays at scale** | Racked surface culture, hundreds of vessels, hand-tended | Tens of grams a month | A hospital ward, triaged hard. Enough to make the university famous and dangerous. |
| **Deep tank** | Stirred, sparged, sterilised fermenters of a few thousand litres | Kilograms a month | A city and its region. This is the point at which the university stops being a laboratory and becomes a government. |

**The transition from tray to tank is the story arc.** It requires the machine shop, the power plant, the sterile-air engineering, the solvent still and the assay lab all working at once — and it converts a scarce miracle into a distributable commodity, which is exactly the moment allocation becomes politics and the hinterland becomes a subject rather than a neighbour.

## Resistance is the clock

The setting has a doomsday clock built in, and it is not a raider army.

Penicillinase-producing *Staphylococcus aureus* appeared within a few years of penicillin's real-world introduction and hospital strains were widely resistant within roughly a decade. The drivers are precisely what a poor polity cannot avoid: under-dosing, short courses, diluted or degraded product, and the hospital as an amplifier.

So **stewardship becomes law.** Plausible institutional responses, all of them fertile:

- Courses dispensed in full and witnessed; not finishing one is an offence against the city, not a private lapse.
- Prescribing restricted to a licensed few — the credential as an instrument of state control over the drug supply.
- Sulfonamides deliberately used first-line for anything penicillin isn't needed for, to spare the strain.
- An isolation ward for resistant cases, and a register of them, because that register is the city's early-warning system.
- A hard ban on the obvious agricultural temptation of feeding low-dose antibiotic to livestock for growth — which someone will be doing anyway, out in the province, and which will be the origin of the resistant outbreak in act three.

And **counterfeiting**, which is historically attested: in postwar Vienna, penicillin was cut or entirely faked and sold at enormous prices, with predictable results for the patients. Chalk, water, and a convincing vial. The city's monopoly on the *assay* is what makes counterfeiting detectable, which is another reason the assay lab is a sovereign institution.

# Part II — The rest of the pharmacopeia, tiered by difficulty

Penicillin is the flagship, but it is not the largest health gain available, and it is not even the easiest antibacterial. Tiering by difficulty is what makes this useful.

### Tier 1 — Pure chemistry, shelf-stable, no biology required

- **Sulfonamides.** Sulfanilamide and sulfadiazine are synthetic, oral, stable at room temperature, cheap, and effective against a broad swathe of infection. They need a chemistry department, aniline chemistry and chlorosulfonic acid — no strain, no fermenter, no cold chain, no sterile suite. **A city-state with good chemistry and bad refrigeration will find sulfa easier than penicillin.** Run it as the parallel track and the primary workhorse, with penicillin reserved. Sulfa's costs are real — resistance again, and rare but lethal marrow and skin reactions — which gives your physicians something to argue about.
- **Aspirin.** Salicylic acid by the Kolbe–Schmitt route from phenol, then acetylation. Undramatic and enormously useful.
- **Ethanol.** Antiseptic, solvent, extractant, fuel, anaesthetic adjunct, currency. The universal enabler; everything else is downstream of a working still.
- **Ether.** Ethanol plus sulfuric acid. This is what makes **surgery** possible, and surgery — debridement, amputation, appendicectomy, caesarean section — saves more lives in a violent, agricultural society than any antibiotic. Ether is also a fire that will eventually burn down part of the hospital.
- **Chlorine and hypochlorite** from brine electrolysis: Dakin's solution for wound irrigation, and water treatment for the city, which outperforms the entire rest of this list on lives saved.
- **Saline and IV fluids.** Salt, glucose, and pyrogen-free water. Arguably the highest-value item in the whole pharmacopeia — rehydration for cholera and dysentery, resuscitation for trauma and burns — and gated entirely on the water problem from §5 rather than on any chemistry.

### Tier 2 — Agricultural and galenical: grow it, extract it, standardise it

Standardisation is the hard part in every case: plant material varies, so each of these needs assay and a house standard just like penicillin.

- **Opium poppy** → morphine and codeine. Analgesia in a trauma-heavy world is not a luxury, and a state monopoly on opium is a plot in itself.
- **Foxglove** → digitalis/digoxin for heart failure. Narrow therapeutic index; standardisation errors kill.
- **Belladonna** → atropine, for premedication, organophosphate poisoning, bradycardia.
- **Willow, ergot** (obstetric haemorrhage — dangerous, valuable), **cinchona** for quinine if the climate permits it or trade supplies it.
- **Yeast and liver extracts** for the B-vitamin deficiencies, plus cod liver oil for rickets. Deficiency disease returns fast in a rationed city, and the cure is agricultural policy wearing a lab coat.

### Tier 3 — Biologicals: needs animals, cold, and sterile technique

All of this is 1890s–1920s technology and therefore very achievable, and all of it is dramatic.

- **Tetanus and diphtheria antitoxin from hyperimmunised horses.** Immunise a horse over months, bleed it, separate the serum, standardise the antitoxin against a reference. Needs a stable, a large calm animal, cold storage and sterile handling. Tetanus in a farming society with no antitoxin is a death sentence; the city therefore keeps a herd of horses whose real job is pharmaceutical, and the horses have names, and the city grieves when one dies.
- **Smallpox vaccine by calf lymph**, and the older arm-to-arm route. Vaccination is the cheapest sovereignty the city has.
- **Insulin** from slaughterhouse pancreas by acid-ethanol extraction. Ties the hospital to the abattoir, needs cold from gland to vial, and makes a small number of citizens permanently and visibly dependent on the state — which is exactly why insulin is a political drug.
- **Blood transfusion**: citrate anticoagulant, glass bottles, ABO typing with locally-maintained typing sera, a donor register. Low technology, high organisation, transforms surgical and obstetric survival.
- **Antisera for local snakebite or anthrax** if the region needs them; same horse, same method.

# Part III — Why the *university* becomes the state

This is the half most likely to get shortchanged, and it is the half the reader is actually there for.

## What the university uniquely holds

A university is not the only surviving institution, but it is the only one holding this particular bundle:

1. **The culture collection** — the strain, the assay organism, the typing sera. Irreplaceable, transportable, and therefore the thing armies come for.
2. **The library** — not just the protocols but the *tables*: solubilities, melting points, dosages, pharmacopoeial monographs, engineering handbooks. Rederiving a table of antibiotic sensitivities empirically costs years and patients.
3. **The instruments and the machine shop.** Balances, glassblowing, a lathe, an autoclave. The shop is what turns knowledge into hardware, and the university's shop is usually the best surviving one outside a factory.
4. **The assay capability** — potency, purity, adulteration, and by extension weights, measures, and coinage. A polity that can certify is a polity that can tax.
5. **A credentialing monopoly.** It can turn a literate teenager into a physician, an assayer, an engineer. That is worth more than any single drug, because it is the only thing on this list that reproduces.

Item 5 is the constitutional one. Charters are paper; the ability to make more experts is power.

## The hinterland bargain

The campus cannot feed itself, so the city-state is a *treaty* with its countryside, and the treaty should be explicit in the setting:

**The province supplies:** maize (for steep liquor *and* for calories), fuel — wood, coal, charcoal — salt, limestone, sulfur or pyrite, hides and tallow, horses for the antitoxin stable and pigs and cattle for the insulin and the vaccine, and above all labour and students.

**The city supplies:** medicine, and the physicians and midwives who administer it; vaccination rounds; veterinary care, which in a farming economy may matter more to a village than human medicine does; certified weights, measures and assay; engineers for mills, water and drainage; and adjudication, because the neutral literate party with no land ends up being the court.

That exchange is what makes it a **regional capital** rather than a fortified campus with delusions. Notice also that the countryside can starve the drug plant without ever attacking it — a single season of planting something other than maize is leverage.

## Rationing is the actual constitution

However the charter is written, the real government is the body that decides who gets the vial. Every allocation rule generates a faction:

- Triage by prognosis (the physicians' answer), by contribution (the guilds' answer), by lot (the populists' answer), by rank (the honest oligarchy), or by payment (the answer everyone denies using).
- Children and mothers first — cheap in drug, enormous in legitimacy.
- Militia and harvest labour first, because those are the people the city cannot do without.
- The **diplomatic reserve**: courses held back not for patients but for envoys, because a case of penicillin V buys a grain treaty, a road, or a neighbour's neutrality.

A vial ends up functioning as **unit of account and instrument of diplomacy** at once. Prices quoted in courses. Tribute paid in vials. And the temptation to debase the currency is literal — cut the powder, and the assay lab is the mint that catches you.

## The decay curve — the two-generation problem

The real threat is not a battle, it is a handover.

- **Generation one** knows how, because they learned it before the collapse. They are also the only people who know which steps in the protocol actually matter and which are ritual.
- **Generation two** learns from generation one at the bench. They are competent and slightly poorer at improvising, because they learned the procedure rather than the reasoning.
- **Generation three** has the book. The book does not contain the tacit knowledge — how the mat should smell on day four, how far to push the extraction before the emulsion breaks, which of the ten steps is the one you must never rush. Yield falls. Nobody can say why.

Hence: **apprenticeship becomes constitutional.** Fixed ratios of masters to students. Mandatory rotation so no single person is the only holder of a technique. A requirement that every protocol be *demonstrated* annually rather than merely archived. Redundant copies of the collection and the library held off-site, with the attendant risk that an off-site copy is a rival capital in waiting.

And the honest version of the decay curve is that it is not monotonic. A generation that loses deep-tank fermentation may keep sulfa and surgery and vaccination and clean water — and be healthier than the city was under an antibiotic monopoly it could not distribute.

## Historical anchors to write from

- **Oxford, 1940–41** — Florey, Chain, Heatley. Bedpans and biscuit tins as culture vessels, custom ceramic vessels when they could get them, the counter-current extractor built from milk churns and a doorbell, the "penicillin girls" tending mats by hand, penicillin recovered from patients' urine and re-injected, Albert Alexander relapsing when the supply ran out. The single best anchor: this *is* a university under resource scarcity doing the impossible thing.
- **Peoria, 1941–43** — the USDA Northern Regional Research Laboratory. Corn steep liquor, submerged fermentation, the cantaloupe strain. This is the scale-up, and it is where a lab becomes an industry.
- **Wisconsin, 1943 onward** — mutagenesis and selection producing the high-yield lineage. This is the strain-improvement programme your city-state can no longer run, which is why the collection is sacred.
- **Postwar Vienna** — the penicillin black market, dilution and counterfeiting, and what it did to patients.
- **The pre-antibiotic ward generally** — puerperal sepsis, rheumatic fever, mastoiditis, osteomyelitis, tetanus after a farm injury. Worth reading for tone, because it tells you what the city is holding the line *against*.
