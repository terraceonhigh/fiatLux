## Why 6–9 sit where they do

- **6 follows 5 immediately** because the pair *is* the device. The reader holds the private argument and the public record and performs the subtraction; a gap between them dilutes it.
- **7 breaks the institutional run.** Five and six are both the machine talking. Diana at seven is the person it is going to crush, and the tonal contrast is the point.
- **8 before 9 is deliberate.** Maia's chapter shows a marriage functioning as a partnership of labour — he builds the clock, she determines its rate, neither half works alone. Putting that immediately before Collie engineers a marriage makes the engineering visible without a word of comment.
- **9 is last** because it needs Lewis in college, which is a time skip, and because it cannot land until Diana has had a chapter.

**The one swap worth considering:** moving Diana to 6 and the minutes to 7. Diana currently appears in ch2 and then not again until ch7 — five chapters, which is a long time to keep a reader invested in someone the marriage plot depends on them loving. The cost is weakening the 5/6 pairing. If the gap since ch2 feels too long on a read-through, this is the lever.

## Hard ordering constraints

1. **Diana's chapter precedes the marriage plot.** She currently exists in one chapter; until she has more page time the marriage plot reads as disposal rather than tragedy.
2. **The minutes follow chapter five.** Six is preferable, seven survivable, later loses the effect.
3. **The marriage plot needs Lewis in college**, so it cannot precede whatever establishes that.
4. **The frats' first mobilisation must exist before ch3**, which is explicitly their second. Strongest candidate is the observatory relocation in ch8's backstory — which would make the first mobilisation constructive and the second an emergency, and would answer the open question rather than adding one. **Note this pulls ch8's *events* earlier than its telling.**
